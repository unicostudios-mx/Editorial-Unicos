# 9. The Death of the Lady

From the Register. Uruk, one thousand seven hundred years ago. And against my will.

There is an entry in this book I have spent weeks circling the way one circles a puddle, and Valeria, who reads these notebooks, has already caught me at it: "You're missing the Lady," she said. "You recount everyone's death except the death of the one who sentenced you." I answered that I was going in chronological order, and it was a half lie, which is the only kind this blue notebook permits me, because it is not the Register. The whole truth is that I did not want to write it. I write it today. In order it belongs here, between the death of Pan and the council of Nicaea, and here it goes.

—

First, for those keeping other ledgers: Inanna was not killed by Rome, nor by a decree, nor by an axe. She was killed by long time, which is the murderer who appears in no chronicle because it works on schedules no chronicler can keep. She was the greatest goddess the land between two rivers ever produced: lady of love and of war, of morning and of evening, the double star. When Sumer went out, she moved to Akkad under the name Ishtar, which was the first great migration my book registered; when Babylon rose, she rose with it; when Nineveh commanded, she commanded too. A thousand years, two thousand, three thousand. The empires bequeathed her to one another like a jewel too valuable to sell.

And then the world changed languages, and scripts, and gods, and the jewel settled slowly to the bottom of the chest. Her great temples closed without any edict, out of pure emptiness. Her festivals shrank until they fit in villages. By the years I am speaking of — the third century of your era — what remained of her was what remains of a fire on the third day: heat in the stones, and here and there an ember where someone still warmed their hands without knowing the name of what had burned.

She sent for me with a dove. The doves were always hers, along with the lions and the star; at the end only the doves were left, which are the cheapest army to maintain. The dove landed on my worktable in Antioch, looked at me with that insolence of the Lady's birds, and I knew.

It took me two months to reach Uruk. I confess here the first shame of this chapter: it could have taken one.

—

Uruk still existed then, though it was already a city with half its body buried in itself: people lived on top of thirty centuries of people, and the Eanna — her temple, the precinct that was the heart of the world when the world fit between two rivers — was a hill of crumbling brick where the town boys went at night to scare themselves, not knowing that scaring yourself there was the last rite the house had left.

I found her at the heart of the hill, in a room that had once been the most adorned on earth, sitting on a remnant of wall, the size of a woman.

The size of a woman. Let the theologians write that down: she who had been the double star fit into a meter sixty of dark-skinned old woman with eyes that still — that much, to the end — had their edge. In her lap she held a clay tablet, and she was cradling it with both hands the way one cradles a fledgling.

"Scribe," she said. "You took your time."

"There was road," I said.

"There was grudge," said the Lady, and the air of the room settled, because before her no one could ever lie either, no book required. "It's all right. It's yours. You earned it. Sit."

And here comes the second shame, and I write it slowly so it hurts as it should: I had rehearsed that scene. For centuries. On the bad nights of my sentence — and in four millennia there are bad nights enough to pave a country — I would tell myself the death of Inanna, and in my versions I arrived late on purpose, or arrived in time and remained standing, or said to her at last, she weak and I eternal, everything one says to the one who nailed you to a book without asking, who gave you immortality the way one hands down a sentence and the second law the way one gives a poisoned gift. I wanted to see her beg. That was my rehearsal, my secret little theater of an aggrieved scribe, and I had polished it so long I knew it by heart.

I sat down beside her on the remnant of wall. The whole little theater came apart in my mouth, and what came out was:

"What have you got there?"

"The last thing they wrote me," she said, and showed me the tablet.

It was cuneiform of the good kind, late, close-packed, a professional hand. An almanac. Star positions, market omens, the year's sky set out in columns. Dated — I read it twice — in a year that in your reckoning falls around 75 of this era. Almost two centuries before that room.

"An astronomer of Babylon," said the Lady. "The last who knew how to write this. It isn't even for me; it's about the sky in general, a paperwork of stars. But my star appears twice." She ran her finger along the signs, with a care I never saw any queen give any jewel. "Three thousand years of hymns, scribe, and my last mention in the script that saw me born is one line in a clerk's table. When he died, the letters died. No one ever again pressed a reed into clay. Do you know what that is? It is having the language in which you were true die on you."

"I know," I said. "I still write in it. In my book. No one reads it but me."

"That is why I sent for you and not for a priest. There are no priests left; and if there were, they would pray to me, and I do not need prayer. I need *reading*." She straightened, a remnant of her true size passing across her shoulders the way sun passes between clouds. "You will do two things for me, Enheduanna of Ur. First, the questions. I know you carry them. Ask them all, hand on your book if you like — that's what you hauled it two months for. I will not die owing you answers. I will die owing you everything else."

I put my hand on the Register. She put hers beside it, and the book acknowledged her the way one acknowledges a founder: without drama.

"The laws," I said. "There were five and you told me three. The fourth cost me Kulla, it cost me Hestia, it is going to cost me all of them. Did you know? Did you give me a book with two laws hidden in it — knowingly?"

"No." The word came out flat, true, and something I had carried a thousand years slid off my shoulders, and I did not know whether it was relief or something worse. "I swear it on what we made together: we said three because we knew three. The book made itself greater than its authors that night, scribe. Everything truly made becomes greater than its authors; that is why we made it with you." She looked at me sideways. "Do you think your hymns say only what you meant? I have spent three thousand years hearing people find things in them. Some are better than yours."

"Why me?" Since I was at it, I went for all of it. "There were a thousand scribes better than I was at precedence, at formulas, at —"

"Because you wrote *I*." She said it the way one reads out a closed account. "The others wrote 'the king did,' 'the god said.' You wrote 'I, Enheduanna' and signed, and the whole sky leaned over to see what that was. A witness is not the one who sees, scribe; seeing, we all see. A witness is the one who puts herself into the record, the one who says: I was there, it is on my account, collect it from me. No one had done that. We needed the one who invented it." And she lowered her voice, and for the first and only time in two millennia of knowing her I heard something in it resembling apology: "We knew what we were charging you. I knew. I did it anyway, because that is what gods do: we solve our hunger with someone else's body and give it lofty names. You call it a sentence — that is its name. I am no longer going to defend myself."

We sat in silence a while. Outside you could hear the new Uruk: donkeys, children, someone hammering. Thirty centuries on top of the heart of the world, hammering.

"And the second thing?" I asked at last.

The Lady of heaven and earth settled herself on her wall, closed her eyes, and asked me for her death in five words:

"Read me what you wrote me."

I knew what. There are no two possibilities. The great hymn, the one from my exile, the one that begins with the crowns and ends with the woman writing: the *Nin-me-šara*, which your scholars keep in their anthologies with my name beneath it, the first signed work of the species, written for her, against my fear, with everything I was at fifty mortal years.

And here the last shame, the one that cost me most to bring to this notebook: I began reading it badly. On purpose. In an inventory voice, flat, the voice of a scribe collecting on centuries, skipping the music, because a part of me — old, small, the little theater still in its hand — wanted her last audience to be a formality. Wanted her to take with her, dying, the bureaucratic version of my love.

It lasted me nine lines.

At the tenth, the hymn overpowered me, as it overpowered me when I wrote it. I have not forgiven it that, nor thanked it, even now; I have spent seventeen centuries undecided, and this book allows me my indecision. I read the rest as it must be read, as one sings to the lady of the morning and of the evening, throat open, in the dead language in which we had both been true, and the words of my youth filled the broken room, and I watched her receive them one by one, eyes closed, the last meal of the first goddess I loved and hated with all my strength, served by the only mouth left in the world that knew the recipe.

She did not go on an important verse. She went in the middle of an ordinary line, a landscape line, and I knew because the room lost a weight it had carried for three thousand years. I finished the reading anyway. All of it. Down to the signature.

Her page closed itself under my hand, opaque and soft, the second of my book, the founder's. The astronomer's tablet stayed in her lap, and there I left it: there are inheritances one does not collect; one keeps them by leaving them.

I came out of the Eanna at noon. I bought bread in the market of the upper Uruk and gave it to the doves, all of it, the whole loaf, and a woman selling dates told me not to feed them so much, they get used to it and never leave.

"They leave," I told her. "They already left."

And I stayed a while with her, helping wave the wasps off the dates, because her conversation was good and I had, that day, nowhere in the world to arrive.
