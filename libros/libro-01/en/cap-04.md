# 4. The First Deicide by Decree

From the Register. Egypt, three thousand four hundred years ago.

By then I had carried the book nine hundred years and believed — the first thousand years one still believes things — that I had seen it all: gods gaining weight with every conquest, gods on rations, gods merging into one another the way two rivers merge, turbulence first and calm after. I had learned the rules of hunger. I still had to learn the rule of the knife.

I learned it in Egypt, which was the worst place in the world to learn it, because Egypt was the country where the gods were fattest.

I am not exaggerating out of old age. In Sumer the gods lived in the temples; in Egypt the whole country was the temple. The river rose and fell with a priest's punctuality, and every flood was read, thanked, recounted. An Egyptian farmer could not plant an onion without feeding half a dozen names in passing. And above them all, by then, was Amun: the hidden one, who began as a local god of Thebes and ended as owner of the empire, with a clergy that administered more land than Pharaoh. I had inscribed him two centuries earlier. I remember the weight of his oath on the page: there are gods who sign like men submitting and gods who sign like men filing one more deed. Amun signed like a bank.

And then a boy inherited the throne, changed his name for that of a god almost nobody worshipped, built himself a capital in the clean desert, where no old god had roots, and decreed.

Your historians call him Akhenaten, and they still argue — rightly; doubting is their trade — whether he was a mystic, a politician suffocated by Amun's clergy, or both, which is what interesting kings usually are. I did not see his soul. I saw the effects, and the effects are in your stones, anyone can go verify them: Amun's temples closed. His revenues transferred. And the crews.

Of everything I have seen in four thousand years, few things have taught me more than those crews of stonecutters going up the scaffolds with chisels, entering the tombs with lamps, to erase a name. They did not burn the temples: they emptied them of the word. Wherever it said *Amun*, a polished hollow. On the stelae, in the cartouches, in the tomb of the king's own grandfather. Chip by chip, an entire empire was written out clean.

I was in Thebes when the orders came, lodged as a foreign scribe — that disguise lasted me two thousand years; it is one of the best — in the house of a priest of the third rank. I watched him read the decree. I watched him look at the wall of his own courtyard, where his great-grandfather had had a prayer carved, and understand that the prayer was condemned. And that night I went down to the shuttered sanctuaries, because that is what I carry the book for, and I found Amun.

The richest god in the world was sitting in the dark of his own padlocked house, listening to the silence eat his revenues. He was still enormous. That was lesson number one: size does not protect; size only lengthens the agony.

"Scribe," he said to me, and his voice was the voice of always minus something, and that *something* was the news. "Does your book register this? Is there a word in your clay for what this boy is doing to me?"

"There will be," I said. "I set down the words when the facts are finished."

"The facts are not going to finish. The facts are going to win." He leaned toward me, and his smell reached me, which was of old incense and now also, faintly, of a house no one lives in. "Understand what is happening, you who last. Men had given us everything: children, wars, famines, slow forgettings. This is different. This is a man killing a god *in writing*. With the same tool you people made us with."

I said nothing. Before me one cannot lie, but I can be silent, and being silent was my way of conceding him the point.

Because that was exactly the new thing. Hunger kills gods the way weather brings down houses: without an author. The decree had an author. A man had discovered — or been prompted; I never knew, and what I do not know I do not write — that faith can be administered: its roads closed, its water cut, the whole of it channeled into a single bed. That to erase the name is to blind the well. In my book that law had existed in reverse from the beginning; it is what they all signed for: to be inscribed is to be witnessed. That year I understood the reverse side that no god had wanted to read aloud: if writing sustains, writing suppresses. Every register is two tools.

And the new god? I went to see him too. I crossed the desert to the king's new city, white, freshly plotted, with streets so straight they hurt. And in the great roofless temple — that was his signature: not dimness but plumb-line light — I looked for the Aten.

Here I must be careful, because these pages admit no lie and the temptation to round things off is great.

I found no one.

I found *something*: a heat that was not the sun's, a vast, faceless attention, poured evenly over the city like the light itself. No body, no voice, no hunger I knew how to measure. The king had written him a beautiful hymn — I have read it in your stones; you can too — in which the god was everything and resembled nothing. A god without image, without family, without stories: a god who was pure *let there be*.

I held out the book and offered inscription, as was my duty with every new god.

There was no answer. Not a refusal: an absence of interlocutor. Like swearing an oath before noon itself.

I noted in my clay, that night, a question I have not resolved in three thousand four hundred years, and I copy it here as it stands, because this book lets me doubt so long as I do not affirm: *are there gods who need no witness? Or are there things that receive worship and are not gods — and then what are they, and what feeds them?*

Much later, other men, in another desert not so far away, would speak of a God without image who shared his altar with no one, and some of your scholars have wanted to run a thread from that hymn to that God. I will not be the one to run it, nor the one to cut it. My trade is to leave the gaps where they are. This chronicle will come, if my courage lasts, to the single blank page of this book, and then it will be seen that with certain absences there is no negotiating.

Of the priest of the third rank who lodged me, I register the ending, because decrees are always recounted from above and always lived from a courtyard. When the court returned to Thebes and the counter-order came, the man — old by then — hired a stonecutter with his savings to re-carve his great-grandfather's prayer on the courtyard wall. He had kept it in memory all those years, reciting it under his breath while he ground his flour, writing it down nowhere, because to write it was to denounce it. And memory, which is unfired clay, returned the text to him with two words changed. I had the original in my notes and kept silent forever, and I set it down here as I understood it that afternoon, watching the old man run his hand over the new signs with wet eyes: they did not give him back his prayer; they gave him back the proof that he had carried it. There are restorations that do not restore the object. They restore the one who lost it. My guild took three thousand more years to put a price on that difference, and it still bills it badly.

And Amun fattened again. He survived another thousand years, until the general hunger took him with all the rest. But he never again signed like a bank. A god who has felt the chisel never again sleeps without a lamp. He told me so himself, centuries later, in his last old age, when he was the size of a thin priest and we were on familiar terms:

"Do you know what the worst was, scribe? Not the hunger. Hunger belongs to everyone. The worst was learning that it *can be done*. That boy taught every king who came after, even the ones who never knew his name, that it can be done. Everything you have seen since, and everything you have left to see — whatever decrees come, whatever bonfires come — will be men discovering, all over again, that it can be done."

And the Aten? The faceless heat went out of the white city when its hymns went out, as silently as it had been present. If he died, he left no body. If he did not die, he left no forwarding address. His page does not exist in my book: there was never anyone to open it to.

Sometimes, in these centuries of stoves and motors, when the noon sun lands on the back of my neck in some ordinary street, I stop a moment, in case there is a vast, faceless attention that recognizes an old colleague.

Nothing has ever happened. I note it down anyway. Noting what does not happen is also my trade.
