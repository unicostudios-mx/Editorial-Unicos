# 36. The Ones Who Did Not Fall

The night after the last rain I did not sleep, and it was not grief, or not only. It is that grief, at my age, comes with its own archivist: the moment I close my eyes, my memory sets about looking for precedents. Three pages closed in one week with my hand laid on; the line halted before the wall; the polite señor waiting on his appointment. My memory was searching, through four thousand years of records, for one that said that something, once, somewhere, had not fallen.

And it found it, because there is one. One. I took it out of the drawer that night — it is a postcard; I will say of what in a moment — and held it in my hand until dawn, and this chapter is that sleepless night, which turned out to be exactly the vigil I needed.

—

In 1954 a family came into my workshop: an elderly señor, his wife, and the teenage grandson carrying, wrapped in a purple furoshiki, a casualty. The señor's name was Nakamura; he had come to Mexico as a young man, with the century; he had built up a notions shop in Guadalajara; and in the war years — this stands in this country's archives, though this country tells it little — the government had forced him, like nearly all his community, to leave everything and concentrate in the capital, for the crime of having been born where he was born. The family remade its life from zero on these streets. The casualty they carried wrapped was a book: the genealogical register of the house, soaked in a warehouse flood, three generations of names running in ink.

I worked that book three weeks with the respect it deserved. And from the first afternoon I knew the Nakamura family had not come alone, because in the workshop, while I worked, there was *someone else*: a small presence, impeccable, supremely attentive, that installed itself beside my table the way a quality inspector installs himself. On the third day, when the family was out, I addressed it with the formulas of my trade.

It was the kami of the Nakamura house. The spirit of their home altar, their kamidana, who had crossed the Pacific with the grandfather inside the luggage of memory — one more way of carrying the sky; my archive already knew the bundles, the scrolls, the songs, and that afternoon it learned the fourth: the sky also fits inside the habit of washing one's hands and clapping twice. He was small, courteous, and exact, and spoke little and well, and over the three weeks of the work we took tea several afternoons, and in one of them he told me the piece of news for which, seventy years later, this chapter exists.

From those teas I also keep the record of the bad years, because he gave it to me himself, unasked, the afternoon we reached the gathering where the war's ink had run worst. When the order came — days to leave Guadalajara, the shop sold off for nothing, permission to carry what would fit — the home altar did not fit. The grandfather took it apart one small morning, tucked the name tablet into the lining of a coat, and made to the hollow in the wall a bow of apology that the kami imitated for me with a precision that hurt: the bow of a man asking his house's forgiveness for not being able to carry all of it.

"A crass error of domestic theology," he told me, with his exact gentleness. "He believed I belonged to the furniture. We do not belong to the furniture, scribe; the altar is an office, not a domicile. I belong to the family. Wherever they set aside the rice, there I hold office." He took tea. "In the capital years, eleven people lived in three rooms, with two other families from the same boat. There was no wall for an altar and no prudence for having one: those were years of drawing no attention. And the grandmother, every morning of all those years, set aside the first rice in a little dish, put it on the shelf of the shared kitchen, next to the other families' spices, and gave the two claps without sound, hands already joined, toward a borrowed wall where there was nothing." He set down the cup, and looked at me. "It was the season I ate best in a hundred years. Note that in your great book, if it fits: devotion without furniture is devotion twice over. A third of my guild did not survive the war, on that side and on this. The ones who survived were those of the families that kept setting aside the rice when setting aside the rice could not be done. The altars did not save us. The stubborn habits saved us."

"In my country," he said, "eight years ago, the emperor declared in writing that he was not a god."

I knew it from the newspapers: January 1946, the country in ruins and occupied, and the emperor publishing a rescript renouncing the divinity the State had built for him; and the occupiers' directives dismantling the entire apparatus of the official cult, the machinery that for decades had made of the sky a government office. In my archive, that carried every mark of the great extinctions: Akhenaten, Theodosius, the Mandate of Heaven. A decree against the gods, and one of the heavy ones: signed by the god himself.

"You have my condolences," I told him, with my guild's formula.

The kami of the Nakamura house regarded me with a perfectly courteous puzzlement.

"Condolences for what, scribe?"

"For the fall. Your heaven lost its throne, its state cult, its official doctrine. In my archive, after that, come the hunger and the closed pages."

And then the smallest household spirit I have ever dealt with gave me, in two sentences, the greatest theology lesson of my modern archive:

"Scribe: the emperor declared that he was not a god. We never declared that we were." He took tea. "The office they closed belonged to men; men had built it, not so long ago, for the business of men. We did not live in that office. We live in the water the señora washes her hands with in the morning. In the two claps. In the rice set aside first. In the grandson — who is from here now, who dreams in Spanish — still bowing to the altar without quite knowing why, and the 'without quite knowing why' *also feeds*, better than you people think." He set down the cup. "Decrees come and go up above, scribe. We were never up above. We were in the middle. Decrees do not reach the middle: there is no address there to serve notice to."

I offered him inscription, out of duty and out of affection. He declined it with an exact bow:

"Being on record is for what fears ceasing to be. We do not need to be on record. We need to *be*. They are different trades, and yours is very honorable, and ours does not require it." And he added, with the only smile I ever saw from him: "But take great care of the family's book — that one, yes. Men do need to be on record. That is why we love them: the fear always shows on them, and it is very endearing."

When the family came to collect their restored register, the grandfather Nakamura wept without sound as he read the recovered names, and the señora gave me the postcard I now hold in my hand: a hand-tinted photo of a torii, one of those red sanctuary gates, planted in the water. On the back she wrote, in Spanish learned by main force: *"Thank you for the names. In my country the gates have no doors. That is why they never close."*

—

That is what the postcard kept telling me all night after Tláloc's rain, and I write it here, on the eve of the chapter that follows, because it held me up for what follows:

My archive is a book of falls. Seven thousand pages of what went out, and this spring, the last candles. But the complete record of the world — the one no book reaches, not even mine — has the other column too, and that night I needed it in front of me: the lords of the sea who crossed sung and are growing. The bureaucrat of the stove still at his post until further notice. The water people of the volcanoes with their offices distributed. The muchacha in the denim jacket with her new book. And the spirits of the country of gates without doors, who did not fall when their official heaven fell, because they never climbed onto it.

Not everything I loved is dying. Only what was inscribed in me.

Write that sentence into my guild with the care it deserves, because it is not bitter: it is a scribe's last freedom. What is dying this week is *my* book — my sewing, my sentence, my seven thousand. The sky — the middle one, the one of washed hands and stoves and Sunday drums — never needed me in order to last.

What a rest, in the end, not to have been indispensable. Now I can go to the wall tomorrow.
