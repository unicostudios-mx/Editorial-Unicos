# 37. The Wall

He came the following Thursday, at closing time, for symmetry, because even the lord of impermanence has his liturgies, and perhaps he above all: when one is the ending of all things, punctuality is the only courtesy one has left.

This time he did not stop the shutter. He waited on the sidewalk for me to decide, and I decided what had been decided since Nalanda: I raised the shutter, lit the good lamp, and set two chairs at the table, because one arrives at the final mournings with the furniture resolved. Valeria was in the back room, under instructions not to come out and with the gas pimienta in her hand; let it stand on record that she obeyed them halfway, which is the exact proportion in which Valeria obeys any instruction to keep herself safe.

Māra came in, saw the two chairs, and something in his kindly tiredness sharpened: he understood he had not come to surprise anyone.

On the table, uncovered, open, lay the Register. Open exactly where we both knew: between page one hundred sixty and page one hundred sixty-one.

"Scribe," he greeted, sitting down. "You finished your wakes. They were..." — he searched for the word with a respect I had not known in him — "correct. The water's, above all. To distribute oneself into one's own rain in front of the payroll. In all my centuries of attending endings, I had never seen anyone leave so much in agreement with himself."

"You didn't come to give me condolences."

"I came to the last page of the matter." He indicated the book with his chin, without touching it. "Let us do the accounts together, as colleagues, which is what we are though it pains you: your book has seven thousand two hundred and three closed pages, one final entry with your name awaiting its line, and *that one*. The one that is no one's. The one that does not eat, does not die, does not sign, and does not let go. You want to finish your book and earn your death; you told me at this table: 'I am going to finish the book.' I want what I have always wanted: that the composite decompose at its hour and without theater. For the first time in this story, scribe, you and I want the same thing. And that page prevents us both."

"Explain to me how a blank leaf prevents you from anything."

"Because the line does not advance." And for the first time in eight hundred years I heard, underneath the rested voice, the edge of something that in any other being would have been called frustration. "You know my rules better than anyone; I told them to you in deeds, from the cement on: I do not choose. I have never chosen. The order was made by your sewing; I only walk the line. That is my one title over your dead, scribe: that it was not I who put them in a line. If I skip it, if I reach my hand over that page toward the last entry..." — he opened his hands, showing the clean palms — "then I am a murderer with preferences, and everything I have been since before your book becomes a lie. Impermanence has no favorites. It is the one decent thing that can be said of me, and I will not lose it over a leaf."

"And the leaf will not yield?"

"The leaf will not yield." He said it looking at it, and across his clear-sky eyes there passed a cloud I took a moment to classify, because I had never seen it on him: memory. "I tried once. Some time ago. I will not tell you when or how, because the result would give you a false idea that there was a struggle. There was no struggle. There was..." — and the lord of all endings, the one who argued with the Awakened One for years on end, chose his words like a man walking on ice — "there was a discourtesy of mine, and a patience that did not fit in my hand. That is all I will say. You have felt it: you sewed it."

"I have felt it."

"Then you know that only one hand in this universe can touch that page." He leaned toward me, and the whole workshop seemed to listen. "The one that sewed it. The book obeys you, scribe: you demonstrated it to me by opening it and by closing it. Tear out the blank page. Finish the census. The line advances, your entry receives its line, you have your death well earned, and I have a world where nothing hangs anymore from a waxed thread. It is my last offer and the first entirely honest one: I ask you for no confessions. I ask you for *housekeeping*."

And there it was. The final temptation, served without venom, which is how the serious ones serve: my own death, the one I had spent weeks earning wake by wake, in exchange for a leaf that is no one's, that does not eat, that does not sign. One empty leaf against the rest of the most tired scribe in history. Any accountant in the universe would have signed.

I took my time. Four thousand years teach you, if nothing else, not to answer ultimatums at the pace of the one who brings them. I looked at the blank page, sewn one night at Nicaea with waxed thread and my best hands. I thought of the empty room of the Temple that made Rome laugh. Of the kind weight of seventeen centuries. Of the hill to the north, the December morning, the barefoot old woman: *there are things that are not written down; that's why we come to see them.*

And I found, at the bottom of the trade, the whole answer, and I gave it slowly, because it was the last record and last records are dictated with all their letters:

"No. And I am going to give you the three floors of the no, Māra, because you have earned them by listening to me for eight centuries without interrupting.

"The first is the guild's. This page is not empty: it is *blank*, which is another thing. Empty is what has nothing; blank is what has not spoken yet, or what decided not to speak. My book does not affirm what it did not witness, but neither does it erase what it does not understand. To tear it out would be to sign, with my hand, that no one is there. And I do not know that, Māra. It is the only entry in my book whose state I do not know, and honest ignorance is filed too: it is filed open. A register that tears out what it does not comprehend is not a register. It is a tribunal, and of those the world below is full already.

"The second is a matter of accounts, and it goes against yours. You say the line does not advance and that my book cannot be finished with a living page. That is your error, and it is old: you confuse finishing with emptying. A census ends when no one is left; but I do not keep a census, though you have called it that in a thousand ways since Nalanda. I keep a *book*. And a book is finished when it has said everything it had to say, not when it has run out of pages. The best books I have restored in twenty centuries end with a question. Mine will end with a leaf that holds everything we did not know, and it will be more complete that way than if it were full. My death will not be given me by your line, Māra. It will be given me by my trade, when I write the last line and declare, as author, that the work is whole. The book obeys me. You just said so yourself.

"And the third is for you, and it is the only one I regret, because it is the only one that will hurt you, if anything hurts you: you are not in my book, and you always believed it was because you eat no faith, because you cannot die, because you are not of my census. That is not why. You are not in my book because you need no witness. You are the only truth in the universe that demonstrates itself, at every instant, in every thing: there is no witnessing you, Māra — waiting is enough. And this page" — I laid my hand beside it, not touching it, as a king taught me with his finger a millimeter away — "does not need you either. You two are my only limits, one on each side: what requires no testimony and what accepts none. Between the two of you fits my entire book, and I with it. There end my dominions and yours. Learn to greet from a distance, colleague. It took me two thousand five hundred years."

The workshop settled into a silence that belonged to no one. Māra did not move for a long time, and in his kindly man's face there worked, slowly, the oldest machinery in the universe processing the only kind of defeat that can touch him: not refutation — he is not refuted; the facts are his — but demarcation. His terrain had been staked out. It had been staked out by a postponed mortal, with waxed thread.

He stood. He smoothed his dustless clothes. And at the door, hand on the shutter, he did what he does: turn the retreat into an appointment.

"The one under the tree demarcated me too," he said, without rancor, almost with nostalgia. "'I see you, Māra,' he would say to me, and that sufficed: to see me is to defeat me, because I work better assumed than seen. You have seen me for eight centuries." He inclined his head — the deep farewell, the gardens' one. "Let your book be on record, scribe. I keep none, but I have a good memory, and in my memory you will last what everything lasts in it."

"How long?" I asked, out of trade.

"Exactly long enough," said Māra, and smiled for the only time in this story, and lowered the shutter himself, from outside, with the gentleness of someone tucking in a child. "We will see each other at the last page."

The steps were not heard leaving, because they never are. Valeria came out of the back room with the gas pimienta still in her hand and her cheeks wet, and looked at the blank page, and the open book, and me.

"Did we win?" she asked.

"Nobody wins against him," I said, and it was the book's truth. "One survives him with dignity, which is different. Put water on for the coffee, doctor. We have two jobs left: a book to deliver and a line to write."
