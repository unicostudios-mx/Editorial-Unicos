# 16. The Deva

The house of Buddhist studies is in colonia Roma, on a street of ash trees, and it is a house like any other on its block except for the silence, which is better swept. There is a room with cushions, a small library, an inner garden with a pirul tree nobody prunes because it would occur to no one, and a man who helps.

The members believe his name is don Sergio and that he is a retiree who drew near the center years ago, "when he was widowed." He waters the garden, arranges the cushions before sessions, repairs what breaks without being asked. The teachers who come from abroad to give teachings greet him with a bow slightly deeper than a retired gardener would explain, and if anyone asked them why, they would say they don't know, and it would be true.

I crossed that garden on a Wednesday afternoon, and the man who was watering set down the hose, shut the tap carefully so as not to waste, and made me the exact bow one makes to a colleague of a different guild.

"Scribe," said Śakra, king of the Thirty-Three, lord of the heaven you have above your heaven. "I've been expecting you since Thursday. Come in. There's tea."

They all give me something to drink. I note it because it matters: four millennia, and those above have never once let me start working without putting something warm in my hands. It may be courtesy. It may be that they know, better than I, that the book weighs less when the hands are occupied.

—

Of all my inscribed, Śakra is the only one who is not entirely mine, and I will explain, because on this depends understanding his death.

The gods of my book live on you: on testimony, on rite, on the just-in-case. The devas do not. Devas live on their own merit: on a capital of good deeds accumulated in previous lives, which lifts them to their heavens the way heat lifts air, and there they burn, brilliant and very long, until the capital runs out. They ask for no worship. They do not need it; technically it does not even suit them: worship is for gods who depend, and they prided themselves on depending on no one. Their doctrine — which is the doctrine of the man who woke beneath the tree, and which describes them with an accountant's precision — says plainly what no other creed ever dared say of its own gods: that they are impermanent. That their heavens are high and are not eternal. That a deva is a lamp burning its own oil, and no lamp comes with infinite oil.

That is why he lasted. When the great hunger took the ones who ate testimony, the devas went on burning from their own reserve, indifferent, and I had their existence on record but not their signatures: there was no reason for a deva to swear in the book of the dependents.

Śakra signed for another reason. He signed the night Nalanda burned, and that story has its own chapter; I advance only the why, in his words from that night: "I have seen the library that best described us burn. I want to be on record in the one that remains."

—

He served the tea in the little room the house lends him beside the kitchen — a cot, a shelf, a photo of the Himalaya cut from a magazine, the exact austerity of one who had a thousand palaces and learned what weight they carry — and sat down across from me, and then, at that distance, with the garden light coming in sideways, I saw it.

I had last seen him thirty years before, and in devas thirty years are nothing: they are even lamps, with no wick to trim. The man in front of me had aged. Not the way you age, in jerks, but the way an ember ages: evenly, inward, with thermal dignity. And in the air of the little room something was missing that had always been in the air around Śakra, which I had breathed for eight centuries without naming, because one does not name what is never absent: that faint fragrance, of flower without flower, which the texts of his doctrine enumerate with the serene cruelty of inventories.

The signs. The five signs of the dying deva. His own books recount them: the garlands wither, the garments stain, sweat breaks from the armpits, the body loses its fragrance, and the deva no longer delights in his seat.

"How many do you have?" I asked, because between him and me circling would have been a discourtesy.

"Three and a half," he said, with the precision of one who has watched it a thousand times in others. "The garlands, years ago; here they were the cempasúchiles of the altar de muertos — they dried on me in two days, the young people blamed the climate. The garments, look" — he raised his arm: on the sleeve of the gardener's shirt, a shadow no soap of this world was going to lift. "The sweat began in March. And the fragrance is leaving — you noticed it just now, don't deny it, you have the face of a scribe measuring a tear." He lowered the arm. "The fifth is still missing. I still find myself in my seat. Still, when I water the garden, the garden and I are in agreement. The day that breaks, count seven days."

"I came to look after you," I said, and even as I said it I knew I would fail exactly as I had failed on Mesones, because those above do not let themselves be looked after by the one from below; the guild does not allow it.

"I know what you came for. And I know about the lady of the fire and the lord of the bricks, and I know who is next in your sewing, and it is not I — so your cot is well placed where it is." He drank his tea. "I did not come to this city fleeing, scribe. I came because this is where one dies best on this side of the world. This people lays flowers for death and bakes it bread. A deva knows how to read a plaza."

Outside, in the garden, a hummingbird was working the pirul. Śakra followed it with his eyes, with a pleasure so clean it hurt, and I understood that he was taking his leave of hummingbirds in general, one by one, unhurried, like a man closing an enormous ledger in alphabetical order.

"I am going to ask you something I have asked no one in two thousand five hundred years," he said then, without taking his eyes off the garden. "And it is the king of the Thirty-Three who asks, so you know the weight: devas die alone. It is the unwritten rule of the heavens. When the signs appear on one of us, the others stop looking at him. Not from cruelty: from dread. We are the longest beings in samsara and the ones who carry its law worst; the dying one reminds the healthy of the bottom line of the contract, and the healthy avert their eyes, and the one who dies, dies in a full palace, alone as a dog. I have seen it done. I have *done* it, scribe. I averted my eyes from friends of twenty thousand years. My memory has been billing me for it ever since."

He set down the tea. He looked at me straight on, and in his retired-gardener's eyes was the rest of it: the lord of the thunderbolt, the king of the mountain at the center of the world, the one who questioned the Awakened One about the origin of thirst and received an answer, the one who has watched entire heavens wither.

"When the fifth sign comes to me — come. Sit where you are sitting now. Open your book to my page, and write my death *while it happens*. Not afterward, as you do with everyone. While. I want to go knowing that a hand is writing line by line, that someone is putting it on record, renglón by renglón." He paused, and his voice came down, for the first time in eight hundred years, to the stature of a man: "I do not want to die alone, Enheduanna. I already know how it is done. I do not want to know how it is done."

"I will come," I said. My hand on my bag, over the book, so the word would weigh what it had to weigh. "And I will write while."

He nodded, the way treaties are signed, and the little room held a moment of that well-swept silence of the whole house. Then he gathered the cups — he is of the kind who gather the cups — and said, with his back to me, in a new tone, administrative, the tone with which a king moves to the next item on the agenda:

"Now ask me what you came to ask and have not asked."

"Who is tearing out my pages?"

"I have felt him twice in this city. The night of the brick and the afternoon of the fire." He turned around. "I have not seen him: I have *felt* him, the way one feels that the rain has stopped. Your people cannot recognize him because your people never dealt with him; we did. He circled the Awakened One for years, disputing with him. He is one of ours and he is no one's. He does not eat faith, so your hunger does not touch him. He is not in your book, so your sewing does not bind him. And he does not hate, scribe; that is what makes him serious. He arrives early, greets everyone properly, gives no trouble."

The words of the neighbor in the bata, exact, in the mouth of the king of the devas. The room went cold on me without changing temperature.

"Tell me the name."

Śakra looked for a moment at the page of the garden, where the hummingbird went on with its work, sewing the air.

"Māra," he said. "His name is Māra."
