# 21. The Return of the Dead

From the Register. Florence, toward 1485.

There is a law of my book that is not among the five, because it does not belong to the living. I learned it in pieces across three millennia — the lamentation off the coast of Palodes was the first piece; I have already told how my book warmed that evening with a heat I did not know how to file — and I finished understanding it in Florence, in a workshop smelling of tempera and poplar wood, the morning I watched a goddess a thousand years dead stand upon a shell.

I will go in order; that is what I have the trade for.

—

I lived in Florence because Florence was, in that century, the best place in the world for my line of work: a city sick with books. The merchants who governed it spent fortunes hunting old manuscripts through the monasteries of Europe, and the hunters came back with mules loaded with Latin and Greek — comedies, treatises, poems that had slept for centuries in damp towers — and all of it had to be copied, collated, bound, restored. There was work for twenty lifetimes. I carried several.

And with the manuscripts, though no one put it in any contract, *they* came back.

Mine. The closed ones. The whole Olympus had lain ten centuries in the opaque pages of my book — Aphrodite closed on me in the fifth century, one afternoon on Cyprus, smelling of salt; Zeus took longer, Zeus always took longer at everything — and suddenly their names were walking the streets of a Christian city again, in the mouths of people who neither prayed to them nor feared them. The poets quoted them. The philosophers of the great house argued them into the small hours, hunting for allegories that would make them decent. The painters sketched them. Nobody, not one soul in all Florence, believed in them; and everybody, absolutely everybody who could read, *frequented* them.

My first diagnosis was the guild's: antiquarian fashion. I had seen it a thousand times; fashions do not feed; the hollow does not eat.

The diagnosis lasted me until the morning of the workshop.

—

I was sent for by a painters' workshop for a minor job: a client wanted a newly copied Ovid bound in good leather, the kind such people gave one another as gifts. I went to take measurements. The workshop was large, with apprentices grinding colors and that exact disorder of places where real work is done, and at the back, on two easels, stood an enormous half-finished canvas that was not much spoken of, because the commissions of the great are murmured.

I saw it sideways first, while I measured my Ovid. I saw it head-on afterward, because there are things that turn a person around.

It was she. Aphrodite — the lady of Cyprus, of the page closed with the smell of salt — standing on a shell, pushed by the winds toward the shore, her hair in her hand and that face which is not of this world because it was of another. The painter had made her from the memory of marbles and verses, without ever having seen her, and he had made her *well*: not the portrait — the air. The air of her. I, who knew her, attest: any of her priestesses of Paphos would have knelt before that canvas without asking the painter's name.

And then I felt it. The book, in my bag, against my side.

Warm.

Not the usual warmth, the even warmth of a hand that lets itself be held. A *localized* warmth, rising from one point of the sewing the way heat rises from a buried ember. I left the workshop with my Ovid and my measurements, turned down the first street, stepped into the portal of a church — the irony did not escape me even then — and opened the Register right there, with hands clumsy with urgency.

Aphrodite's page. Closed, opaque, soft, dead as she had been dead a thousand years.

And warm.

I spent that evening and that night with the book open on my table, taking temperatures like a madwoman nurse. Warm: Aphrodite's, Zeus's, that of Minerva who had been Athena, Mercury's twice over. Cold: their same-century neighbors whom no poet had yet dug up. Faintly warm, Pan's — and it came to me that a bookseller had mentioned, that very week, new verses about the goat-footed god running the hills of somebody's eclogue. Cold, Serapis's, whom nobody quoted. The correlation was perfect and the guild would not let me deny it: **the pages of the dead warmed exactly in the measure that the living told them.**

And in the middle of that night of temperatures I remembered, with a guild shame I set down in full, that the book had already warned me once and I had not listened. Decades earlier, in the years when the codex hunters were coming back from the northern monasteries with loaded mules, there had passed through my hands the copy of a Latin poem newly unearthed from a library in German lands, where it had slept for centuries: the poem of the Epicurean, the one that teaches that the world is atoms and chance, that the gods — if gods there are — live far off, serene, unconcerned with us, and that fearing them is the source of all misery. It was given me to assess for the state of the paper, and I read it entire, out of trade and out of pleasure, because it is written with a beauty that does not keep company with its doctrine. And I note now what I noted absently then: the poem that denies that the gods concern themselves with us *opens by invoking a goddess*. It opens with a hymn to the lady of the shell, mother of the Romans, delight of men and gods, without whom nothing comes forth into the shining shores of light. That working afternoon, with the codex under my hands, I felt in my bag a small warmth I blamed on the brazier. It was not the brazier. It was she, receiving her first hot meal in five hundred years, served by the man who had come to give her notice of eviction. Men are like that, and let it stand in the law I was about to write: even to say the sky is empty they must first sing to someone. That is why the dead last.

They were not coming back. Let that be clear, because here is where astonishment becomes doctrine and doctrine must be written with a steady pulse: no page opened. None could be written on again. The dead of my book stayed dead, and what was reaching them was not faith — no one in Florence ever asked that Aphrodite of the canvas for anything, or feared her, or left her flowers. What was reaching them was another thing, thinner and perhaps deeper, the same thing that fed the lamentation of Palodes: *being told*. The banquet of the dead, served a thousand years late, in the world's new language: art.

That night I wrote in my notes the sixth law, the law of the closed, and I copy it as it stands:

*The gods have two lives. The first feeds on faith and ends. The second feeds on telling, and I have not yet found where it ends. Mythology is not the cemetery of the gods: it is the country they emigrate to.*

—

In the months that followed I performed an experiment that shamed me, and I set it on record for that very reason. I chose Aphrodite's warm page, laid my hand upon it, and spoke to it — I, who do not speak to closed pages, who carry my mourning with my hands and not my voice. I asked her whether she wanted to come back. Whether the heat was enough to desire with. Whether there was, on the far side of the opacity, anyone whom my sewing could still serve as a door.

The page did not open. I did not expect it to open. But the heat, under my palm, stayed still and full, the way afternoon sun stays on a wall, and in the guild of those who touch paper there are readings that need no ink. I wrote it thus, and thus it stays:

*The well-told dead do not want to come back. They are better off than we are: no one can stop loving them too soon anymore.*

—

I end with the part that matters to this story, the reason this chapter is sewn here and not in the chest of my anecdotes.

Five hundred years after that Florentine morning, one night this spring in a workshop in the Centro, I said to Valeria Ibarra: *publish it as fiction. Mythology is how the dead survive.* She believed, I think, that it was a strategy — and it was a strategy; I have told my quartermaster's reasons, the experts, the oblivion on a schedule. But underneath the strategy was Florence. Was the warm page under my hand in the portal of a church. Was the sixth law, verified across five hundred years in every museum, in every school edition of the myths, in every child who learns the name of Zeus without praying to him:

A published book is a dining hall for seven thousand.

When you read this story — this one, the one in your hands — somewhere in the world a very long table is being set. I do not ask you to believe that. The book is yours on record; the rest is my archive's business. But if, closing this chapter, you find yourself holding a name in your head for a moment — the brick one, the fire one, the doorman, the lady of the shell — know that the moment is not lost.

In my guild nothing is lost. That is why the bag weighs so much.
