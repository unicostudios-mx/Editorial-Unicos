# 15. The Island of Isis

From the Register. Philae, year 537.

When the empire closed the temples, there was one place where the order took one hundred and forty years to be carried out, and it was an island the size of a sigh, south of the south of Egypt, where the Nile enters the world through the first cataract. It was called Philae, and it was the last working pagan temple on earth. Let the arithmetic stand on record, for it is among the stubbornest my book keeps: Theodosius decreed in 391; Rome fell entire; the empire changed capital, language, and shape; and on Philae, every morning of those one hundred and forty years, the priests of Isis went on waking the goddess with the same rite as always, as if the world outside were a rumor of merchants.

It was no miracle. It was a treaty, and treaties are also my guild, so I register it with pleasure: south of the island lived the Blemmyes and the Nobades, desert peoples Rome never fully tamed, who loved Isis with that frontier faith which is the hardest of all to kill. The empire, no longer in the market for cheap wars, signed with them a curious peace whose terms your own chroniclers wrote down: the peoples of the south could come up to the island to worship, and once a year could borrow the statue of the goddess, carry her upriver to consult her, and bring her back. Read it again: in the Christian empire, by written clause, a goddess went on annual tour among her last faithful. Isis was the only one of the great ones who died with a collective bargaining agreement. She never stopped being the cleverest.

—

Because that is what Isis was, and my book says it with numbers: the cleverest. The other great ones were gods of one people; she was the first *international* goddess. When Egypt lost its throne forever — the last pharaoh born of that land was called Nectanebo, and after him there were only foreign managements — the other great ones of her house shut themselves in their temples to administer nostalgia. Isis did the opposite: she learned Greek. She let herself be sculpted in the current fashion. She packed. In the centuries when her country was a province, she opened houses in Athens, in Rome, in the islands, in the ports of Gaul, in Britannia — I have seen her worshipped under the northern rain by legionaries who could not have pointed to Egypt on a map. The goddess of the occupied country became the lady of her occupiers' seas. No strategist above or below has ever bettered that move.

From her good centuries I keep one record that seems to belong to another book, which is why it goes in this one: the afternoon I first saw, in the necropolises of the north, the industry. For the faith of Egypt in its great age was not only temples: it was manufacture. Beside the sanctuaries of the sacred animals there were workshops in due form, with suppliers, with wholesalers, with a high season: the pilgrim arrived, bought an embalmed ibis or a falcon or a cat according to the counter, dedicated it with his name and his plea, and the priests carried it down into the galleries, where your scholars have counted the deposits by the millions. Millions — doubt it who will: there are entire catacombs, excavated and counted. I went down into one with a priest-administrator who showed off the storeroom the way one shows off a full granary, and back in the light I asked Isis, who was mistress of a good part of the business, what my trade obliged me to ask her: whether it fed. Whether testimony manufactured in series, bought at a window, counted the same as prayer on the knees.

"You are confusing the wrapper with the offering, scribe," she told me. "The ibis is made by the workshop, true. Sometimes it doesn't even hold an ibis: the workshops cheat — feathers go in, a bone goes in, stuffing goes in; I know it better than you and I lose no sleep." She arranged her hair, with that gesture of hers of having the accounts already done. "But the one who walks three days from his village, saves his coins, chooses the bundle, says his name and his sick mother's name, and leaves it on my steps believing he has given it to me... that man did not buy a bird. He bought a way of reaching me. The intention arrives whole, whether it comes wrapped in linen or in a workshop's lie. We charge the intention. The fraud is a problem for the customs office below; it does not come upstairs."

I noted it then as a guild curiosity and understood it completely two thousand years later, in a cathedral with hymns by decree: the line dividing what feeds from what does not, does not run between the artisanal and the industrial, nor between the true and the fraudulent. It runs between what is given willingly and what people are ordered to feel. A stuffed ibis bought with faith has weight; a perfect hymn sung by decree weighs nothing. The scale above reads one thing only, and the workshops of Saqqara and the committees of Paris stand, in my archive, on either side of the same law.

I visited her three times in those final years. The third was the closing.

—

In the year 537 of yours, the emperor Justinian, who was making a clean copy of the world, finally ordered the old decree executed. A general came with troops, without cruelty and without haste, to do paperwork: the priests arrested with bureaucratic courtesy, the images inventoried for shipment to Constantinople, the doors of the sanctuary sealed. Your chroniclers dispatch it in two lines. I was on the island, with my book, among the last pilgrims from the south who were no longer allowed up, and I attest that the two lines are exact and that inside those two lines fit the end of four thousand years.

I found Isis the evening before, on the terrace that looks toward the cataract, the size of a woman of the south, her hair braided in the fashion of the Blemmyes, which was her last fashion. On the wall behind, among thousands of pilgrims' names scratched into the stone, there was an old graffito, in a priestly hand, already one hundred and forty years old then: the last thing anyone ever wrote on the island in the sacred script of Egypt. She touched it now and then while we talked, absently, the way one touches a beloved scar. Of that graffito and its author I say no more here: they belong to another book of this archive, and archives, like goddesses, know how to keep turns.

"Scribe," she said to me. "You came for the closing. How professional we both are."

"I came to ask what you are going to do."

"It is already done." She pointed with her chin at the river, southward, where the campfires of the turned-away pilgrims dotted the bank. "I go with them. Upriver there are chapels of mine for two, three generations more. Then that too will end, and I will not complain: four thousand years, scribe. No contract above has ever paid so well." She settled her braid. "My page — close it yourself when it is time, without warning me. I hate long farewells. They are for gods who never traveled."

We talked all night, the last one. About Serapis, her husband by commission — "poor thing; they manufactured him to be my consort and he turned out a better merchant than I; I wept his last twenty years of jokes — did you know at the end only the mockery still fed him? I was fed by the pleas of women in labor, which is a better diet." About the question they asked her for five hundred years, which she repeated to me looking at the river, because she knew my book collects exactly those questions:

"Do you know what they asked me more than anything else, scribe? It wasn't about harvests or children. It was: *why didn't you defend us?* When Rome, when the edicts, when they closed the house in Alexandria. Why didn't the lady of the ten thousand names come down and break the soldiers' arms." She was silent the length of a small flood. "And I could never answer them the truth, because the truth is not said to a believer on his knees: that I was great *out of them*. That my arm was their faith gathered, and when their faith broke with fear, my arm broke with it. We die of you, scribe. That is not forgiven and not explained. It is carried."

On the morning of the closing, the soldiers brought the images down the landing stairway, wrapped in canvas, with that carefulness of men who do not believe but just-in-case. The priests walked behind, in single file, with what they wore. Last came a very old priestess, the dean, who had served on the island since girlhood, and as she stepped onto the ship's gangplank her foot failed her — I saw her tip sideways, saw the Nile below, quick and without patience, saw the soldiers too far away.

She did not fall. She straightened in mid-air, the way reeds straighten, with no one touching her, and boarded the ship, and nobody noted it down because there was nothing to note: an old woman stumbles and recovers herself — it happens a hundred times a day in every port in the world.

I noted it. I was watching Isis, on the terrace, and I saw her pay for that straightening: saw her lose all at once the color of a century, saw her grip the wall with both hands and then stand very still, breathing, like someone who has just carried a piano alone. A whole century of her four-millennia reserves, spent so that an old woman would not get wet or crack a hip, on the last day of the last temple, without the old woman ever knowing.

The gods could no longer defend anyone. They could, sometimes, with everything they had left, straighten an old woman on a gangplank. In my book, since that morning, those two sentences are worth the same.

—

Her page closed sixty years later, on an ordinary afternoon, alone under my hand, while I worked in Antioch. It smelled faintly of lotus when I touched it, and then not. Upriver, her last chapels went out with the generation she had said, not one more and not one less: to the very end her accounts came out right.

And I register one last thing, with the care with which one walks another's temple, because my book does not affirm what it did not witness:

Years after the closing I passed a church of the new Egypt, of the desert monks' kind, and on the wall there was a painted image: a mother with her child in her lap, giving him the breast, with large front-facing eyes, in the manner in which that land has painted eyes since always. Your scholars have spent a century debating the resemblances and inheritances of those images, and I will not be the one to settle the dispute: my trade has already said what it does with what it does not know. I only leave on record what I did, which I do know: I stayed a while, offering no inscription to anyone, taking no measurements of the air with my scribe's science. People came in, left flowers, prayed for their children. The mother on the wall looked back at them with her enormous eyes.

I left without conclusions and bought bread. There are days when that is the whole of the trade: look, do not affirm, buy bread.

Her people called her the lady of the ten thousand names. I keep a book where only one of them fit, and four thousand years later I am still not sure the world isn't calling her by one of the other nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine.
