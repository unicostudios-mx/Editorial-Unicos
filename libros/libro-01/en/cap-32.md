# 32. The Rehearsal

Two days after the refused pact I went to the house of cushions, because the book woke me in the small hours with the deva's page warmer than its account, and because after saying no to the one who is never wrong, a person needs to sit with someone who has won at least one similar argument.

Śakra was in the garden, sitting on his little bench, not watering. That was the first piece of news: the hose coiled, dry, and he looking at the pirul with his hands on his knees. Four signs and a half. The seat still agreed with him, he said, but it agreed "the way a borrowed chair does: it can be done, but you notice."

"They tell me," he said, no preamble, because dying kings do not spend, "that the one who disputes came to see you and you told him no."

"Did they tell you right?"

"He told me himself." He smiled at my face. "Don't give me that archive look, scribe: we visit. We are the two oldest in the district; Sundays aren't enough for so much youth. He came, sat where you're sitting, recounted your triple no with a precision my own chroniclers could envy, and do you know what he said at the end?" He settled himself, savoring it. "He said: 'The demarcation was clean.' From the one who lost every round with the Awakened One, that is a decoration. I'll translate it in case your guild doesn't handle the currency: you did well. You said no to the one who is never wrong, and only those manage it who have a book to finish."

"I came to see you, not to collect medals."

"You came so I'd tell you you're doing fine, which is different, and I've told you. And a good thing you came, because I was going to send for you. We have work, you and I. We must rehearse."

"Rehearse what?"

"My death," said the king of the Thirty-Three, in the exact tone with which the house announced tea. "Do you think a six-day vigil improvises itself? I have presided over ten thousand ceremonies, scribe. The ones that go well are the rehearsed ones. I am not leaving my final act to talent."

—

And we rehearsed. I set down the rehearsal complete, because it is the strangest and most useful afternoon of my four millennia, and because someday someone of my trade — the heiress, or another scribe in another century, with another book — will have to accompany to his death someone who already knows how it is done, and the king's protocol will serve her.

He sat me on the bench where I was to sit — "this one, not the other; from the other you're backlit and I want to see your face without spending on turning my neck." He had me open the Register to his page and time how long it took me to wet the pen, "because between line and line I want no ink silences; I know ink silences: they are when the scribe is crying and doesn't want it noticed."

He dictated the instructions one by one, with a gardener's calm and a court's precision:

That I write in the present, as agreed, line by line, and that if my voice trembled when I read aloud, I was not to fix it: "the trembling is also part of the record."

That when the last minutes came I was not to take his hand. — Here he stopped, and explained it looking at the pirul, because there are instructions not even kings can give face-on: "Touch is the first thing to go out on us devas, scribe. It leaves hours before the rest. If you take my hand at the end, I will no longer feel it, and I will know it is there and that I do not feel it, and I will leave with that last piece of news: that the world let go of me before letting go. Do not do that to me. Read instead. Hearing is the last thing to go out in all the beings of samsara; that is in our books and it is true. Read to me past the point when you think I no longer. A while longer. That while is the one that matters."

That no incense be allowed into the little room: "incense is for altars, and I am dying an employee, not an altar."

And that the last line, the one that comes after, be mine to decide. — That too he said to the pirul: "The record is yours from where I no longer dictate. There your true trade begins, the one no god can rehearse for you. I trust your last line, Enheduanna. I have read your face for eight hundred years: you write best when nobody commissions you."

Then he dictated me the program. That is what he called it, and he drew from his shirt pocket a small paper folded in four, written in plant-marking pencil, because the king of the Thirty-Three, who governed a heaven with a thought, made the itinerary of his death in gardener's handwriting on the back of a nursery list.

"On the first day I am going to talk to you about my palaces. Don't celebrate it and don't pity it: it is inventory, and inventory is received with an inventory face. The second day is yours: I am going to ask you your whole life, with method, and you are going to answer me, because it is my vigil and at my vigil the conversation is what I say it is." He raised a hand before I could open my mouth. "Four thousand years listening and writing things down, scribe. Someone has to interrogate you before the world ends, and it is going to be a king, and it is going to be in my best week. It is my one great whim. Grant it to me."

"And the third day?"

"The third I do not announce." He folded the little paper again, with archive care. "There are chapters that, if rehearsed, are no longer spoken. The fourth I will sleep; don't waste it: write the fourth. Nobody has written the sleep of a deva, and your lines will come out well with no witness. The fifth I will ask for the window." — Here his gaze went to the garden, and took its time, and I noted nothing down, because there are pauses that are not ink's business. — "The fifth is for the garden. I have pending matters there. Matters of names."

"And the sixth?"

"The sixth is not rehearsed," he said, with the first roughness of the afternoon, which was not aimed at me. "On the sixth day I will be busy."

He was quiet a moment, and then I saw the thing none of the ten thousand ceremonies had ever required: the king searching for words. He found them where the doormen and porteras of this book find them, in the small:

"Only one thing won't fit in the program," he said. "The hummingbirds come to the toronjil every morning, at the same hour, with that punctuality of carrying a heart at a thousand. And the house is good, but they water when they remember. Who is going to tell them?" He looked at me, and for the first time in eight hundred years the lord of the thunderbolt made me a request he did not know how to make. "There is no procedure for that, is there? I checked. No heaven has one. It is the one thing this post does not let me delegate or take with me: the pending business of the small living. Now I understand your Hestia with her lentils. Let it stand for her, wherever things stand for her now: at the end, I understood her."

I told him I would take charge of the toronjil. It is the only lie I have ever told a god with my hand near my book — it was not a lie yet; it was a promise without a how, which is different, and besides I have kept it: ask the house who comes on Tuesdays to water. He nodded, satisfied, like a man crossing the last item off the nursery list.

—

At the end of the rehearsal, as I was leaving, he stopped me with a gesture and pointed at the pirul.

"Cut me a reed. From the low branch, the one that grows straight." And when I hesitated: "It is my garden, scribe. It still obeys me."

I cut the reed. He held it out to me with both hands, in the manner of the serious presentations of his land.

"The kings of old gave elephants," he said. "I had thirty thousand and I miss none of them. The kings of now have gardens. Make yourself a pen from this. You know how; your first trade was reeds, you told me so at Nalanda." He closed my fingers over it. "I want my death written with something I watered. And whatever you write with it afterward — whatever it is, even your own last things — will carry a little of my garden's water. That is all the eternity a deva can aspire to, and look at that: it is enough."

The pen is on my table as I copy this. With it his record was written, line by line, six days after the rehearsal, all according to his protocol, without one ink silence he had not authorized. With it there will be written, when the time comes, the thing this notebook has already announced will be written.

I left the house of cushions at dusk. At the door, the serious teacher caught up with me and asked, quietly, whether don Sergio was all right, "we see him somehow dimmed." I told him the truth of mortals, which is the truth with the scale changed: that he was finishing a very long piece of work, and finishing it very well.

"Yes, isn't he?" said the teacher, looking toward the garden. "That's what I thought too. I don't know why it gives you gladness and sadness at the same time."

Because that feeling has a name, teacher, I thought as I walked down the street of ash trees with my reed in my bag. In my guild it has had one since Sumer. It is called being in the last pages of a good book.
