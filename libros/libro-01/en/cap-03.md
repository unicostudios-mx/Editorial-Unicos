# 3. The Morgue of Impossibles

The Institute of Forensic Sciences sits on Niños Héroes and smells the way all temples smell: of cleaning product barely winning against the thing it means to clean. Valeria got me in as an "external expert in historical documents and materials," a title that exists whenever she pronounces it with sufficient annoyance at the correct functionary. I watched her work the paperwork with an admiration I didn't confess: bureaucracy is the last ritual this city has left, and Val officiated like a priestess.

"Ten minutes," said the physician on duty, a young man with three shifts under his eyes. "And touch nothing, because this one has worked enough miracles on us already."

He said it without knowing what he was saying. That is how they always talk.

Kulla was on table four, under a sheet somebody had folded with more care than regulation requires. The young man with the tired eyes drew it back and kept his distance, arms crossed, like a man turning his back on an open window without deciding to close it.

Four thousand years of knowing him and I had never seen him asleep. Gods do not sleep; now I know it was the one thing they were missing. He had his hands on his chest, those broad hands with which he taught me to stamp a seal into fresh clay the night he swore. His face wore that tired peace the photo had already told me about. He was the size of a small man, and of a density the table acknowledged: the metal gave one millimeter too many, as under a stone.

"The results," said Val, leafing through a file she had not been given permission to leaf through. "Tell her, doctor."

The young man sighed.

"There's no genetic profile. Not degraded — *absent*. The cells have nuclei and the nuclei have... nothing my instruments recognize. Radiocarbon on tissue: between four thousand and forty-five hundred years, in a body with yesterday's lividity. Strontium isotopes in the teeth: this gentleman drank, all his life, water from a river which, according to the map they gave me, is the Euphrates." He closed his folder. "And the cement. Did they tell you about the cement?"

"The bricks," I said.

"They're gone. This morning the pour woke up normal. Ordinary concrete, ordinary set. We have last night's photos and today's material, and they don't match, and the field examiner is seriously considering reporting that the photos were corrupted, because that's the only word that fits in his form."

That too I have known forever: the tracks of the gods are like the gods. Without fresh testimony, they come undone. By Friday the whole file would begin to embarrass the people who wrote it, and within a month it would be an anecdote nobody would tell sober. Oblivion needs no conspiracies. It is the natural slope of the world, and that morning I felt the whole grade of it in my knees.

I stepped up to the table. The young man began the gesture of preventing me and did not finish it; there are gestures that dissolve near certain dead.

"May I?" I asked anyway, because rituals matter.

I took his right hand. Cold, with that texture of polished clay it never lost, not even in his best centuries. They had cleaned the jobsite out from under his nails and that hurt me in a specific way: it was the first time in four millennia that Kulla had no work under his nails. Between thumb and forefinger, where the young examiner had not looked because his form has no box for it, I found what the Register had already told me in its own way.

Fibers. Three, four. Short, twisted, of a beige that is not of this century. Rag paper, linen and hemp, sized with a gelatin recipe that stopped being used when people stopped believing a book should outlive its owner.

Paper from the Register. His own page.

Either it had been placed in his hand, or he had managed to catch hold of it. I don't know which of the two possibilities was worse: the killer as officiant, delivering to the dead man his own certificate — or my friend, in the last second, grabbing onto the only faith he had left, which was one line in my handwriting.

"What is it?" Val, at my side, phone in hand, flashlight on. She misses nothing. She never misses anything, and that is her worth, and it was my problem.

I looked at the fibers. I looked at my friend — because she is one; at my age one no longer confuses the words — at her historian's face smelling a document about to change a bibliography. And I lied.

"Modern cellulose," I said. "From the sheet, probably."

It came out of my mouth with the ease of an ancient trade. I can lie anywhere except in two places: upon the pages of the book, and to anyone who questions me swearing upon it. In the rest of the world, four thousand years give you a fluency no polygraph could catch. I am not proud of it. I am saying how it is.

Val held my gaze one second too long. I registered it, because registering is what I do, and in her column I noted: *she didn't believe me; she decided to wait.* In mine: *I lied to her; I decided it would be the last time.* Of the two notations, I already knew which would cost me more.

"Doctor," she said without turning, "what happens to him now?"

"Nothing good. Nobody claims him, nothing identifies him. Seventy-two hours and he goes to the common grave with a number."

The common grave. Kulla, lord of the brick, master builder of the first wall, would enter the earth of this city the way rubble enters it.

"I'll claim him," I said.

They both looked at me.

"With what kinship?" asked the young man, already reaching for the form.

"Acquaintance. I know it isn't enough." I buttoned my coat. "Doctor: no one else is coming. You know it with that strange certainty that's been bothering your stomach since yesterday and won't fit on any of your papers. When the deadline runs out and the judge asks, tell him there was an elderly lady willing to pay for a burial. Judges understand that. It is one of the few things this century still understands."

Outside, in the parking lot, Val caught up with me. She planted herself between my person and the avenue, which is her way of asking questions.

"Edna. A body with Euphrates water in its teeth turns up drowned in cement ten blocks from your workshop, set into bricks with a seal you identify on sight, and you claim him for burial. I tell stories of dead religions for a living. I recognize one when it's standing in front of me."

"Then you know that stories have an order," I said. "And that telling them before their time kills them."

"Is that a 'later, I promise'?"

"It's a 'later, I promise.'"

She let me pass. As I said: she decided to wait. People think patience is a soft virtue. Val's is an edged instrument, and I walked to the Metrobús stop feeling it on the back of my neck.

On the way home I did my sums, which is what I do instead of praying.

Someone had reached the Register without forcing my safe: that demanded an explanation and I had none. Someone had torn out Kulla's page: that demanded strength, of an order I did not yet want to name. And someone had left — or returned — his page in Kulla's hand: that demanded something worse than strength. That demanded *ceremony*. Thieves do not officiate. Madmen do not fold the sheet.

Whoever killed my friend understood divine deaths. Had seen them. Had, perhaps, attended them.

And there were six living pages left in my book, in an order I knew by heart because I sewed it with my own hands: after the brick, the fire of the hearth.

Hestia.

That night I didn't eat. I took out paper and ink, the good ones, the fine-work ones, and began the list I had not made in a century: where each of the remaining ones was. Halfway through I stopped, went to the back room for the Register, and set it beside the paper, open at the torn stub of page eleven.

"I am going to find out who it was," I told it, because after four thousand years one speaks to one's sentence with familiarity. "But first I'm going to warn them. You taught me that too, Kulla: first you shore up, then you investigate the collapse."

The book, warm, said nothing. Outside, the garbage truck passed with its bell, which is the closest thing to a temple ringing that my street has left.
