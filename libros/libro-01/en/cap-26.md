# 26. The Manufactured Goddess

From the Register. Paris, 1793.

I have watched gods be born. It is not frequent, and it is not how they paint it: a new god does not arrive with trumpets but the way damp arrives in a wall, little by little, by capillarity, until one day the stain has a face. Serapis was the exception of my census — born by commission and viable even so — and for two thousand years I held him to be the only successful case of a manufactured god.

In Paris, in the autumn of 1793, I saw the other case. The one that failed. And I learned the difference, which it was going to hurt me to understand one hundred and twenty years later, the week I myself tried to manufacture a miracle by decree.

—

I had lived in Paris since the old king's years, restoring books for people who bought them by the meter. When the Revolution came, I stayed, against the advice of all my centuries. Revolutions are bad for bodies and extraordinary for my trade: under no other circumstance can a scribe watch, in real time and in a single city, an entire people renegotiate its contract with the sky.

And this one renegotiated like no other. It did not change gods, like converts. It did not hide its own, like the defeated. This people *dismissed the staff*. In two years I watched the churches of Paris close the way businesses close in a bankruptcy; I watched the bells melted down for cannon — the metal that called to Mass going out to kill Prussians, which is the most theological industrial reconversion I have ever witnessed; I watched the saints on the portals of Notre-Dame beheaded by chisel, because the citizens took them for kings, and in a way they were not wrong: they had been collecting at the same counter for centuries.

The ones above of France — the small ones, the local ones, the guild virgins and bridge saints still alive after the great hunger — understood nothing. They sought me out at night, as they always seek me when the ground shakes, and asked me what those above always ask: *is this persecution, or is it forgetting?* Because persecution is resisted and forgetting is negotiated, and they are opposite strategies. And I did not know what to answer them, because it was a third thing that neither they nor I had ever seen: it was *planned substitution*. Akhenaten's decree, but voted in assembly.

The one I remember most from those nights is the old man of a ford of the Seine, a small lord of small waters who had spent centuries collecting at a saint's window — he himself no longer remembered which; long loans end up as property — and who came to my workshop in the middle of the Terror, with his smell of silt and his boatman's dignity, to ask me the question with the same two words as all the rest. I answered him what I knew, which was the third word, the new one, and he weighed it a while with his head down, the way water folk weigh things, who think everything by the weight of what the current drags. "Then there is no one to talk to," he said at last. "A persecutor you resist. A forgetting you outwait. But a *plan*?" I could not answer him that either. He asked my permission to warm himself at my stove, stayed an hour without speaking, watching the fire the way one watches a more fortunate colleague, and took his leave with a courtesy from the time of the sainted king. His page closed that winter, on its own, under my hand, one night of ice when the river ran high and loaded and no one on the new bridges lit a candle to the crossing. It smelled of silt when I touched it, and then not. In my census he stands under the only title he had for four centuries, which was borrowed, and under the one he had for a thousand years before, which was his. He was among the last small ones of France. The small ones always go first: they are sewn to the world with fewer stitches.

In August of that year, before the Reason, I watched them inaugurate a fountain on the rubble of the Bastille, for the great festival of the unity of the Republic. They raised her in plaster, provisional like everything sworn eternal that year: a seated lady between two lions, in the Egyptian manner, water flowing from her breasts, and the president of the Convention filled an antique cup and drank, proclaiming sacred waters of regeneration. They called her Nature. I stood at the back of the crowd with my book under my shawl, studying the statue's still face and front-facing eyes, because I had known that lady the size of a woman of the south, her hair braided in the fashion of the Blemmyes, taking leave of her island with her accounts in order. Dead twelve centuries, and the men who were dismissing the entire staff of heaven could find, for the premiere of their world without gods, no better mold than hers. That night, in my workshop, I opened her page for the first time in a long while. Warm, faintly, the way my dead warm when someone tells them. The cleverest, even dead: this is what the ten thousand names were for. One of them dies on people, and the other nine thousand nine hundred ninety-nine keep working for her.

On the 10th of November 1793 — they said the 20th of Brumaire, because they had overturned even the calendar, and that was the sign I should have read first: whoever refounds time means business — I went to Notre-Dame with half of Paris. Inside they had built a mountain of cardboard and cloth over the choir, with a Greek shrine at the summit and a torch of Truth. The musicians of the Opéra played. And at the culminating moment, out of the mountain came Liberty: a beautiful singer, in white, in the red cap, who seated herself on a throne of greenery to receive the homage of the city.

The Festival of Reason. It is in your books; the details I give are all on record. What is not on record is what I went there to do: I went with my book under my shawl, as I go to every birth. Out of professional duty. Just in case.

And here comes the part that costs me to write, because it is the part where my trade touches its own floor:

Something began to set.

I felt it midway through the hymn, when ten thousand voices sang to Liberty with wet eyes — and there were wet eyes; it was not all theater; it is never all theater, that is exactly the problem. Something began to condense in the air of the nave, above the shrine, where things always condense: an attention returned, a nascent weight, the damp stain looking for its face. My book grew warm. I have attended three divine births in four millennia and I know that heat: it is identical to a page about to be written.

It lasted as long as the hymn.

When the music stopped and the singer came down from the mountain to greet the deputies, the thing that was beginning stayed up there, alone, unfinished, the way breath stays on glass. And I remained until the cathedral emptied, watching that almost, the book's warmth cooling, and I understood why Serapis had lived and this was not going to live.

Serapis was manufactured by a commission, yes. But afterward the commission stepped aside, and the god was left out in the open weather of real faith: the sick who climbed his hundred steps just in case, the sailors who left him coins with no one watching. The just-in-case, that small coin, is true faith because nobody administers it: it is given alone, without witnesses, without decree. The Reason of Notre-Dame had hymns by commission, festival by official calendar, and devotion by act of the Commune. Everything visible of a cult and nothing of the invisible. She was a god of pure noon: no shadow where the believer could doubt alone, and therefore no shadow where he could believe alone. And what is not believed alone does not feed.

The law was dictated to me by that fogged glass, and I write it as I noted it that night, because one hundred and twenty years later I had to read it back to myself with shaking hands:

*Administered faith does not feed. What people are ordered to feel has no weight.*

—

The rest went quickly, and it is all on record. The Hébertists, fathers of the Reason, were guillotined in the spring: the cult was orphaned at four months old. Robespierre, who found the invention atheistic — revolutionaries have theologians too; they have the most — manufactured his own: the Supreme Being, with an enormous festival in June, designed by the best painter in France, with Robespierre as celestial high priest lowering an effigy of Atheism in flames. I went to that one too. With the book. Out of duty.

That time nothing set at all. Not even the breath on the glass. A hundred thousand people on the Champ de Mars, and the air above dry as sandpaper, because by June fear had eaten everything: people sang glancing sideways at the commissioners who noted who sang, and fear — let it be said once and for all — feeds many things in this world, but it has never fed a god. The gods of fear in my book ate *free* fear: fear of the mountain, of the night, of the sea. Guarded fear does not rise; it stays at ground level, among men, which is where it does its work.

Seven weeks later, the head of the high priest of the Supreme Being fell into the same basket as the others, and his god-by-decree did not wait for him a single night: there is no page to close on one who never reached a page.

And the Reason? The almost, the one of the fogged glass, my damp stain without a face?

I searched for her for years, with my trade's lamp turned low, through the nights of Paris. I never found her, and I never found either the clean emptiness left by those who never were. There remained of her — there remains still; I verify it every time this century argues at the top of its voice — a particular exposure: a cold place in the air of assemblies, exactly the size of a goddess, where men set statues that do not weep and capitalized words that do not answer. I heard them swear by Her for the next two centuries, in parliaments and on barricades, with a faith many in my census would have envied.

They pray to the hollow. The hollow does not eat. That is the whole difference.
