# 40. Record of the Witness

*(Epilogue by Dr. Valeria Ibarra)*

I stayed till the period. There was no period. Those who have come this far have seen how the previous chapter ends: on an open sentence, half a word short of an apology she had no need to ask. As her editor, it fell to me to decide what to do with that ending, and I decided what she would have decided: nothing. One does not complete a scribe's last line. It is published as it remained, which is how true things remain: unrounded.

She died on April 17th at seven forty in the evening, with the first rain of the season at the window. The death certificate — there is a certificate; I saw to it; we of the world below keep our papers on record — reads Edna Aranda, restorer, ninety-one years old, cardiac arrest. The ninety-one were estimated by an overwhelmed forensic physician, and it is the only figure in this whole story that makes me laugh: he was short by four thousand two hundred years and change. We buried her in Dolores, in a plain grave, on a sunny Thursday. We were few: the coordinator from the house of studies; doña Chela of the tamales — she doesn't know why I invited her; she came because "the señora was a good customer"; two neighbors from the workshop street; the senior mayora of the volcanoes, who came down without my telling her, "because the water said so"; and a muchacha in a denim jacket who kept to the back, very still, very respectful, and who was not among those present when I tried to count her twice.

The bricklayer who sealed the grave — I swear it by my rigor, which is the only thing I swear by; I checked his name afterward with the cemetery administration and he does not work there, and nobody hired him — set the last brick of the curb, leveled it, gave it a little tap with the handle of his trowel, and looked at it for half a second with something he did not know was faith. His nails had jobsite under them. I never saw him again. He kept a promise from 1907; in this story debts are paid slowly, but they are paid.

—

Of the book I must render accounts, because that is what she left me heir for, and because accounts are rendered even when no one asks.

The Register is safe, and I will not say where, and I ask you to appreciate the irony that the historian who fought for the status of truth is now the one who administers a secret. Let us say it is where books last: in company. I consult it for my work with the gloves she taught me not to use — "the bone of the wrist, doctor; gloves are for people who don't know where their fingers are" — and every time I open it, it is warm, like a hand that lets itself be held. I know the thermal explanation. I note it in my notebook every time, out of method. It convinces me less every time, out of experience.

This book, the one you are finishing, I transcribed from the blue notebook without touching a comma that was not orthographic. I offered it as its author disposed: as a novel. My agent found it a house; a publisher of this country took it; and in the process there occurred the one thing that obliges me, as a professional witness, to leave a formal record of anomaly: when we discussed the book's interior, nobody at the publishing house objected to the blank page. Not the editor, not the proofreader, not the printer. The leaf goes blank in every edition — you just turned past it, between the chapters where it belongs — and when at the final meeting I asked who had approved that page, they looked at one another, and the head of production, a man with thirty years in the trade, said exactly: "Well... it was always like that, wasn't it?" I noted it in the notebook with date and time. It is the shortest record I have and the one that weighs most.

—

I am asked — I will spend my life answering it, and I accept the post — whether all of this is true.

I answer as a historian, which is what I am and what she asked me to go on being: the verifiable facts, I verified. Enheduanna existed; her disc is in a museum in Philadelphia; her hymns are studied; the first signature in history belongs to a woman. The cult of the graniceros exists and works the seasons of the volcanoes to this day. Sahagún's complaint about the hill of Tepeyac is in the appendix to Book Eleven of his History. "Great Pan is dead" is in Plutarch. The five signs of the dying deva are in the Pali canon. The year of hunger is in this city's archives, with its dead who got no record. All of that you can go verify; the bibliography exists; a good part of it I know by heart because I checked it crying.

And the unverifiable facts are unverifiable. That too is a result — the most honest one of my career. For ten years I collected impossibles under the label *Miracles*, waiting to debunk them; now I administer the most impossible archive in the world and I no longer wait to debunk anything: I wait to *understand*, which is a longer wait and better accompanied. My teacher left me the method one afternoon in a morgue, and it works for books and for grief: stories have an order, and telling them before their time kills them. I tell them at their time. This was this one's time.

—

I end with the report on the world, because she would have asked for it this way: what was done, what was decided, what comes next.

In colonia Doctores there is a new building where once there was a concrete pour with an impossible dead man in it. I went by last week. The bricks are well laid — I know how to look; an expert taught me — the courses even, and at the site they told me it was a building without a single accident, "rare thing, señito." In the Juárez, the building on Marsella changed management; the new tenants complain that sometimes, coming home from a bad day, they find the stove lit, one low flame. The complaint never goes anywhere. On Mesones, the vecindad hired a new doorman, an older señor who asks "who?" with an intonation that made me stop on the sidewalk for a full minute. It may be a habit of the trade. It may be that trades find hands. I no longer hurry to decide.

On the hill to the north, the line of those who believe does not end. I went in December, with my mother, who had been asking me for years. I did not go in to dispute anything: I went in to watch her pray, which is a thing a third-generation atheist can do with full rigor if she learned, from the best source, that witnessing is a form of love. On the way out I bought a veladora. I keep it in the workshop, next to the portrait of a bookbinder I never met. I do not light it. There it stands, just in case, and I now know, thanks to four thousand years of data, exactly how much a just-in-case feeds.

And somewhere in this city, a muchacha in a denim jacket carries a pale-covered book with three hundred blank pages and a single line written on the first, which I have not seen, but which I know by heart, because I knew the woman who dictated the clause: *This book has an exit door.* Those who know about these things say — and now the one who knows is me, no help for it — that it has not yet found its scribe. That it is in no hurry. That about waiting it knows more than anyone.

Sometimes, on workshop nights, when I finish a restoration and the paper lies breathing under the lamp, I think about the vacant post and I stand still a moment, with nothing to read, feeling a kind weight.

Then I turn the page and go on working. I was taught that this is how it is done: doubt with your hands busy.

It is the adult form of faith.

*V. I. Mexico City, April.*
