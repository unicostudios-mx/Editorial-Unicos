# 2. The Sentence

From the Register. Ur, four thousand three hundred years ago.

Historians know four things about me, and all four are true. That my name was Enheduanna. That I was the daughter of Sargon of Akkad, the king who first stitched the cities into an empire, and that he made me high priestess of Nanna, the moon, at Ur, to bind the south with a daughter instead of a garrison. That I wrote hymns, and that in one of them, for the first time in the memory of the world, someone wrote *I* and signed her name. And that when a man called Lugal-Ane rose against my house, he tore me from the temple and cast me into exile, and that afterward I returned.

What they do not know is what fits inside that "afterward."

Consider what the world was then. There was no world: there were cities, and each city was the visible body of its god. Ur was Nanna. Uruk was Inanna. Nippur was Enlil, and to say "Nippur has grown" and to say "Enlil has eaten" was to say the same thing. The gods did not live in the sky; the sky was small. They lived in the temples as fire lives in the lamp, and we were the oil, and we knew it, and no one was scandalized, because the lamp also gives the flame a place to be flame.

My father united the cities with the army. He never understood — or understood too well, I will never know — that above, the same thing was happening as below. That when Akkad subdued a city, its god ate first at the sacrifices of the conquered. That an empire of men is a diet of gods. The theologians who came later called it syncretism. I saw it up close, and I assure you it looked less like a wedding than like an annexation.

I got the part below first. Lugal-Ane entered the precinct with armed men. He took my tiara, took the name of my office, put me out through the door the dead go out by. I wrote a hymn about it that is still studied: *he made me fly like a swallow from the window*. Scholars admire the metaphor. It was not a metaphor. That is exactly how it feels, and I was the only person in the world, then, whose trade was to say in the first person how things feel.

That is why they chose me. Not for holiness, not for wisdom, not for being a king's daughter.

Because I had invented the witness.

—

They found me in exile, on the salt flats where the banished wait, between supplication and the pen, for the world to decide whether it wants them back. They came by night, though for them the night was a courtesy, not a necessity.

They did not come as flames or as storms. They came to scale, which was their way of being polite among themselves: person-sized, their shine badly hidden, like embers under ash. I saw the Lady of Uruk with her horned crown, and she was not my lady — I served the moon — but anyone would have known her: the air around her had an edge. I saw lords of cities that were already my enemies. I saw gods I did not know, thin ones, with a short shine, and I understood without being told that they were the gods of the defeated cities, and that if they were there, seated in the same circle as the fat ones, it was because things above were worse than things below.

It was the Lady who spoke. Inanna. The one my hymn had exalted above all others, in verses that now looked back at me from her mouth with an irony I preferred not to measure.

"Scribe," she said — and she did not say priestess, and in that one word fit everything that was coming. "We are at war and we cannot afford to be. If those of us above fight the way those below fight, the oil spills and the lamp goes out for everyone. We need treaties. Treaties need oaths. And oaths" — she made a pause that had been going on since before I was born — "need a witness before whom no one can lie."

"You have a thousand witnesses," I said. "You have the whole sky."

"We have one another," said a thin lord, with a laugh that had no humor in it. "Ask any treaty how well that works."

"We lie," said Inanna, simply. "We lie the way you people breathe: without deciding to. We are made of what men believe, and men believe contrary things before breakfast. We need something we are not. We need fixed memory. We need" — and here she looked at the rush bag where I carried my tablets, my work, my *I*, the only thing Lugal-Ane had not been able to take — "someone who has already done, alone and for no one, what we need done for everyone."

I could tell you I resisted. I carry a law that forbids me to lie in these pages, so I will not tell you that. I was a banished woman of fifty on a salt flat, stripped of temple, office, and city, and the kings of this world were not answering my letters. And the queen of the other world stood before me, offering the one throne no one had ever offered anyone: the witness's seat.

I asked a single thing:

"And when I die? What good to you is a memory that dies?"

They looked at one another. I know how to read that look now; I didn't then. It was the look of those who have already discussed the price behind closed doors and are waiting for the other party to sign without reading.

"You will not die while the book is open," said Inanna. "That is the second law."

She said it to me the way one bestows a gift. That the gods do not understand what they give is not wickedness. It is that they have never carried a year, and I was going to carry four thousand three hundred of them, one by one, with all their dead inside.

I said yes.

—

The book was made that night, and I did not see how. I saw the before: clay from the flats, my scribe's reed, a bowl of water. I saw the after: the first tablets, which weighed what tablets weigh and were warm as a hand. In between there is a moment my memory — absolute since that night; it is part of the sentence — keeps blank. It is the only blank I have. I learned to be grateful for it.

The laws were three, they said. Over the centuries I discovered they were five, and that the two they didn't tell me were the ones that mattered. But that night they were three: no one lies before the book. The scribe does not die while the book is open, nor can she destroy it. And to be inscribed is to be witnessed — the book counts as the last believer of every name it keeps. It was for that third law that they all signed. No god signs out of love of order. They signed because a book that does not forget is the only temple that cannot be sacked.

Then they wanted to swear, and the argument over precedence began: who signed first, whether the order of the pages was hierarchy or mere list, whether a lord of storms could come after a lady of childbirth. It lasted three days. I swear it by this book, and you know by now what that is worth: three days, banished on a salt flat, watching the owners of the sky fight over the order of a line, and it was the first time I thought — not the last — that they resembled us because they were made of us, and that this was at once their glory and their diagnosis.

On the third day, a small god who had been sitting at the edge of the circle stood up, dusted himself off with both hands — broad hands, a workman's hands — and said:

"I'll sign wherever. I have a job half done."

His name was Kulla. He was the god of the brick: of clay cut, molded, dried, and bedded; of the plumb wall and the even course. The great ones laughed. He waited out the laugh the way one waits for mortar to set, without hurry, and when it was over, he swore first of his kind, upon the fresh clay, with a builder's seal he drew from his belt.

The great ones took, of course, the first ten pages. Kulla got the eleventh and never minded.

While he stamped his seal he looked at me, and he was the first of all of them — the first of the seven thousand to come — who looked at me the way one looks at a person and not the way one looks at a new piece of temple furniture.

"You will outlast us all, scribe," he told me, quietly, without sadness, with the professional certainty of a man who knows his materials. "Fired clay outlasts flame. It is not a curse. It is manufacture."

And he went off to finish his wall.

—

Of the rest, the tablets of men tell the truth: I returned from exile, recovered my temple, served the moon and died in its time, and they carved me a calcite disc that sits today in a museum, with my figure at the head of a procession. The dead woman of that disc was me and was not me. There was a body to bury; do not ask how, because that answer belongs to the Lady and she never put it in writing, and what is not in the register — I learned it from her — was never decided.

I left Ur by the door of the living, with two donkeys, forty kilos of warm clay, and a new trade.

For the first century I thought my work was war: treaties, oaths, the frontiers of the sky. In the second century I understood the truth. The treaties were the pretext. My work, the real one, the one no god said to my face on the salt flats, was the other:

Someone had to write down the deaths.

And above, though that night not one of the signatories would have admitted it before the book, they all already knew. There is a reason one invents a memory that does not die: because one has begun to suspect that one does.
