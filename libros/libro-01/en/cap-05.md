# 5. The Census of the Living

The list took me the whole night, and not because it was long.

Seven thousand two hundred and three names my book holds. Alive, at dawn that Thursday, there were six. I set them down here as I set them down on my paper, in the order in which they swore, because the order is going to matter more than anything else in this story:

*Hestia*, fire of the hearth, sworn in Greece when Greece was villages. She lives — lived — in colonia Juárez, as portera of a building where she lights the stoves of the lonely.

*The Lar*, last of the lares of Rome, god of threshold and pantry, sworn under the Empire. A doorman too — the household gods understand porterías; it is the same trade — in a vecindad in the Centro, on calle de Mesones.

*The blank page.* It is not a name. It is a place in the book. Of that page I will speak when its turn comes, not before.

*Śakra*, lord of the Thirty-Three, king of the devas, sworn the night Nalanda burned. The only one in my book who does not live on you: he lives on his own accumulated merit, like a lamp burning its own oil. That is why he has lasted. That is also why his dying is different, and of that I do not want to speak yet either.

*Huehuetéotl*, the old fire, lord of the center of the hearth, sworn in 1521 among the rubble. He lives in the embers of a comal in the San Cosme market, and I say *lives* generously: for decades now he has been more memory than fire.

*Tláloc.* Sworn in 1521, after the old fire, because that is how he wanted it: "water enters after the flame, so as not to put it out." The most alive of all who remain. The only one people still pray to by name.

Six living, a killer who was already at number two on the list, and a scribe who had not paid a visit in one hundred and eleven years.

I began at the end of the line, and I am not ashamed to explain why: Hestia and the Lar stood first in the order of danger, but Tláloc was the only one who might tell me something I didn't know. The small gods see their street. Tláloc sees the water cycle.

—

To reach him you leave the city by the south, let the sprawl fray into workshops and tlapalerías, and climb. There is a point on the Ajusco highway where the air changes owners: below, it belongs to the cars; above, to the bunchgrass and the firs. There I got off the colectivo, at a spot with no name for the driver and three names for me, and walked.

I found him where I always find him, in the clearing of petitions, with the people of the water.

Val's scholars call those people graniceros, and they have written whole dissertations about them, almost all respectful, almost none sufficient: men and women touched by lightning, who survived it, and who from then on work the weather — they ask for rain, move the hail along, negotiate with the airs on the high hills, with dressed crosses and offerings of food. Anthropology files them as "survivals of pre-Hispanic cults with Marian syncretism." I file them differently: they are the last active payroll of my book. The clergy that remains.

There were nine of them that day, carrying flowers and mole up the slope in market bags, and among them, hauling two nets of cempasúchil like one more laborer, went a short, solid man in a palm-leaf hat, with the stride of someone who does not tire.

Not one of the nine knew whom they were helping carry the flowers. That is how he works now. The great ones of old demanded the believer's face turned upward; the survivors learned the logistical humility of the delivery man.

He saw me from far off, the way he always sees me: without surprise. He said something to the mayora of the group, handed her the nets, and came down the slope to meet me, and the bunchgrass straightened behind his step, which is one of the few vanities he allows himself.

"Scribe."

"Lord of the water."

Up close he smells of wet earth. Not "like" wet earth: it is the original smell, of which yours is the copy the soil makes when rain touches it. The chemists of this century gave the phenomenon a laboratory name. That's fine. Naming things is how you begin saying goodbye to them.

"One hundred and eleven years," he said. It was not a reproach. It was a measurement, of his kind: he counts time in rainy seasons. "And you come with the book in your bag and death on your face. Who?"

"Kulla."

He took off his hat. He stood a moment looking at the valley below, the brown smear of the city that used to be a lake, and I respected the silence because I knew what he was doing: he was searching his memory for all the walls that brick and water had raised together, which is all the walls there are.

"How?" he asked at last.

I told him everything: the cement, the torn page, the fibers in the hand. He listened the way his people listen, without interrupting, letting the account finish raining before deciding what to plant.

"Torn out," he repeated. "The page." He looked at me straight on for the first time. "Not just anyone can do that. That book of yours does not permit it, scribe. I watched it refuse the fire of an entire temple. Who has the hand for that?"

"I don't know. I came to ask whether you do."

He shook his head slowly.

"The ones who had that strength are all in your closed pages." And then, without changing tone, with the same calm with which a storm is announced: "But strength is not the first thing that needs explaining, and you know it. The first thing is the door. To tear out a leaf, the book must be closed. Open, it defends itself. Closed, it is a book." He put his hat back on. "You closed it, Enheduanna."

My name. The real one. No one had spoken it since 1915.

"I closed it," I said. Before him, with the book in my bag, there was no other way to say it, and in any case I didn't look for one.

"You already knew," he said, and it was not a question. "We have been going out since you closed. Slowly, evenly, in order, the way the candles of an altar go out when the feast is over and the sacristan makes his rounds. I have felt it in the rains for a century: every year what used to cost nothing costs a little more. I told the old fire and he said: that's age. We gods lie to ourselves with that one too, you see? We had to resemble you in something."

The wind came up from the valley smelling of distant asphalt. The nine of the water, above us, were beginning to lay out the offering in the clearing, and one of them, the mayora, whistled softly at the sky, a work whistle, the way you let the boss know the table is set.

"Why did you never hold it against me?" I asked. "A hundred years, and not one of you came to say anything."

"Hold what against you? That you were tired?" He shrugged. "Scribe: we built you the sentence. We are not going to bill you for the wear and tear too. The one who made you the book with no exit door was..." — he stopped, chose the word with an old man's care — "was us. Besides" — and here, I swear it upon these pages, he smiled, that mineral smile I have seen three times in five hundred years — "dying little by little is not so bad once you've seen everything. The bad thing is the other. What happened to Kulla. To be killed before your own water — that is a rudeness."

"I am going to find out who it is."

"You will find out," he conceded, like a man confirming a forecast. "And when you do, you will have to decide something harder than a name." He adjusted his cape. "That day, come up. It will rain, so you can think better. Thinking in the dry season — even gods are bad at that."

He turned and climbed back to his people, a short gentleman in a hat among nine people with market bags, the last great god of this continent going off to arrange flowers with his clergy on foot.

And I went down the mountain with what I had come to find and would have preferred not to: the killer had not opened any door.

The door had been open for one hundred and eleven years. I opened it.

On the colectivo back, wedged between a boy with a gas tank in a backpack and a lady with gladiolas, I took out my paper and did what I do: I made a clean copy of the census and dated it. Then, on the line below, I wrote the sentence that was going to accompany me through the rest of this story, and I copy it here without correcting a word of it, because that is how it was written:

*Order of events: first I closed. Then someone decided that closing was permission.*
