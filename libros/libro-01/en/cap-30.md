# 30. The Century of Silence

From the Register. 1882–1915.

In 1882, a German professor with failing eyes published a book with a scene you people quote much and read little: a madman lights a lantern at high noon, runs to the marketplace crying that he seeks God, and when the ones there — who no longer believe — laugh at him, the madman gives them the news: we have killed him, you and I. And then he looks at their faces and understands, and says the sentence that took my breath away in the Leipzig bookshop where I read it standing up: *I come too early. This event is still on its way.*

Your theologians have spent a century and a half debating that page as blasphemy or as prophecy. I read it as what it was, because in my guild one recognizes the genre by the first paragraph: it was a dispatch. An advance correspondent filing the report of a war I had then been covering from the front for four hundred years. The professor was not announcing a deicide: he was *drawing up the record* of the great hunger, the final one, the one that was no longer any Theodosius's edict but something worse, something without author and without decree: the general noon. A world ever more lit, measured, explained, insured, railroaded — and the ones above, who had always lived in the shadows of human uncertainty, left without a single corner in which to be necessary.

The nineteenth century was that, seen from my table: the century in which the lamps killed more gods than all the axes. I spent it burying. Every decade, pages closed on me by the dozen, alone, quietly, without even a body to claim: little ford gods who died when the iron bridge crossed their river; ladies of the fever who died of a vaccine; spirits of mill, of forge, of the king's highway, swept off by the turbine, the blast furnace, and the railroad. Nobody persecuted them. The world simply stopped having holes their size. My book grew fat with dead and the sky grew thin, and I began to do the arithmetic no scribe should do: how many are left to me, and how much of this is left to me, and what for.

In 1893 I took ship for Mexico. I can give the professional reasons, and they are true: it was the country of the great move, the only place in the world where my census still had a living colony of survivors, hidden in the hills and in the customs, and I wanted to spend my last pages near my last living. But under the professional reasons was another, simpler, which I have confessed only here: I was tired of the continent where I had buried most. We millennial ones flee too. Only we call our flights moves.

I set up the workshop on the street it still keeps, with another name then and other paving. And in 1898, one March morning, there came through that door a woman of thirty-one with a gutted book in her rebozo, carried the way one carries a sick child.

—

Her name was Amalia Reyes. She was a bookbinder in the shop of a printer on the calle de Santo Domingo, daughter and granddaughter of printing people, and she brought an eighteenth-century missal her employer had given up for dead. "They told me you resurrect books," she said. "I don't resurrect them," I answered, with the pedantry of my centuries, "I keep them company so they last." She looked at me a moment with dark eyes full of serious mockery and said: "Well, that then. Like the angels, but with paste."

Four thousand years, and I was disarmed by one line from a bookbinder.

I am not going to recount here what we were, because there are pages that belong neither to this book nor to anyone, and because modesty is the last thing left to a scribe who has put everything else on record. But there is one thing from those years that this book needs to know, because this book is written *upon* it:

At Christmas of 1903, Amalia gave me a notebook. She had made it herself, with her binder's hands: good paper from her reserves, French sewing, boards covered in a blue she chose "because you always go around in archive colors, and the archive deserves some sky too." She gave it to me wrapped in papel de china and said, with her serious mockery: "It's for when you want to tell me something long. I don't know what. You'll know. I only made you the space."

She spent a month making it for me, in secret, at the bench next to mine.

I never wrote in it. Twelve years I kept it in the drawer of my table, perfect, waiting, and every so often she saw it there while hunting for an awl and said nothing, and that not-saying of hers was the most patient question anyone has asked me in four millennia. The space was made, with its covers of sky. The one who was not made was I.

Those who keep this book's accounts already know in which notebook what you are reading is written. Yes: in that one. I took one hundred and twenty-three years to break in the space she made me, and I broke it in to tell something long to the whole world except her. Let the arithmetic of that sentence be carried by this archive, because I can no longer carry it alone.

One single day of those seventeen years I will tell, because Valeria, editor of this notebook, says it needs more of Amalia, and because it is the day that best explains the entire century. The May night of 1910 when the city went up to its rooftops to watch the comet pass. It is in your newspapers of the time: Halley was coming with a tail of poison, said the alarmists, and there were men who sold pills against the end of the world, and people who boarded their windows, and people who confessed twice just in case. We watched it from the workshop roof, between Amalia's flowerpots, with pan dulce and a veladora she lit "just because."

"And is that from above, Edna?" she asked me, very serious, face lifted toward the tail crossing the whole city's sky. "You who know about old books. Is it someone?"

I put my hand on my bag, on the Register — I still carried it everywhere in those years; that habit had five years left — and there was nothing: no new warmth, no signature, no hunger. A stone with vapor, punctual as the trains, crossing a sky that two hundred years earlier would have filled with processions and that now filled with telescopes and charlatans' pills.

"It's no one," I told her. "It's a rock that comes back every seventy-some years. I've seen it pass before." And the count came out of me on its own, aloud: "Fifty-some times."

Amalia looked at me a long moment, the veladora between us, and did not ask what that count meant, because her not-asking was her way of holding the door for me. Then she looked back at the comet and said the sentence I chewed on for the rest of the century:

"Poor thing. All that travel and nobody kneels anymore." She sipped her canela. "Though it's better, no? That they watch it without fear. Things of the sky must get so tired of all that fright. It must be a rest, to be found merely beautiful."

The sixth law, whole, spoken by a bookbinder on a rooftop. I had discovered it in 1485, with a warm book in a church portal and months of taking temperatures; Amalia understood it in 1910, in one sentence, having read nothing. That is how she was. I kept the census of those above; she understood those above by the guild of below, the way she understood books without Greek and without Latin: by the spine, by the sewing, by the manner of being broken.

I will say what is needed to understand the closing, no more: that we worked together seventeen years, her bench beside mine, her paste and my thread; that she never knew who I was and knew it better than anyone — "you have hands that have buried a great deal," she told me one night, asking nothing, and laid her hand on top of my hand, and I let that gesture last longer than was prudent and less than was just; that with her I learned the one language I was missing, which is the present tense; and that the seventeen years I spent at her side are, in a life of four millennia, the chapter I reread.

And that the city starved her.

—

You study it as "the year of hunger," 1915, and your historians have it well documented: the capital passed hand to hand between the armies of the Revolution, each with its own paper money that the next declared garbage; corn beyond reach, the trains carrying soldiers instead of wheat, typhus riding on hunger as it has always ridden. People with money ate badly. Working people did not eat. Books, that year, nobody bound: they were sold by weight, by the kilo, for wrapping or for the fire, and two women who lived by keeping them company watched the oldest trade either of them had turn into butcher paper.

Amalia fell ill in March, of typhus, her body already mined by months of eating little so the nephews could eat. I nursed her the way one nurses when one has watched the whole world die for four thousand years: without clumsiness, without hope, and without rest, which is the worst of the ways because it leaves nothing to reproach yourself with afterward, and reproach, I had learned in my nineteenth century of burials, is the last form of the embrace.

She died in April, at dawn, my hand in her hand and her pulse going the way a sewing thread goes: even, meekly, down to the knot.

And here I must register the part it took me a century to look at straight, which is the true record of the closing:

I did not close the book for Amalia. If only. A grief is a reason; reasons are honored. I closed for what went through me three days after the burial, when I returned to the empty workshop, saw the Register on its stand, warm as a hand, waiting for me as it had waited four millennia, and felt — for the first time in my whole sentence — disgust.

Disgust at my trade. Disgust at my table. There stood the book that holds seven thousand deaths from above, the book that had eaten my entire life line by line, and there was not in its seven thousand pages one single place where it might be on record: Amalia Reyes, bookbinder, thirty-one years of mocking seriously, dead of the greed of men in the year of hunger. My book does not register mortals. It never did. I had spent four thousand years noting in fine ink the agonies of the immortals while around me, in every century, on every street, the people died without a record — the people who light the stoves, and bed the bricks, and paste the spines with paste. The people who, said with the exactness this book demands of me, *manufacture the gods and are on record with no one*.

"And mine — who writes mine down?" I asked the Register out loud, in Spanish, because it did not deserve the first language. "Eh? The ones below — who?"

The book, warm, said nothing. It registers. That is its part.

I wrapped it in its linen. I put it into the German safe I had bought new four years earlier, when the world still had a remedy. And I said two syllables, without ceremony, without witnesses, without knowing that three laws were five and that the two they had not told me were the ones that mattered:

"No more."

—

That was the closing. One hundred and eleven years later, a polite señor in a hurry called it, on a corner of burnt paper, "starting it." And the book I now write — this one, the one you are reading, the one I opened to tell everything — exists because both sentences are true at once, and because it took me three dead gods, an atheist historian, a girl goddess, and a three-minute downpour to understand what Amalia had been telling me since the first day, with her gutted missal in the rebozo, and which I leave here as the record of that April, a century late:

Books are not closed because one is tired of reading them.

They are closed when they are finished. And mine, in 1915, still had seven living pages, one of them blank, and its scribe, who by then knew everything about grief, did not yet know the one thing grief actually teaches: that letting go too soon and never letting go are the same error, gripped with a different hand.
