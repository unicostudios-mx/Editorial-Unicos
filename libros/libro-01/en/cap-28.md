# 28. The Mandate of Heaven

There is a god in this city to whom I owe a sixty-year apology, and this chapter is the apology.

I met him in 1963, on calle de Dolores, which is this capital's Chinatown: one tight block of cafés and shops where the Chinese community of Mexico — which arrived with the century, worked like no one else, and suffered what this country has still not finished telling itself — planted its piece of world. I went for rice paper for a restoration job. I came out with a god.

I felt him from the sidewalk: small, supremely orderly, living above a café de chinos, in the kitchen of a family. I went up with the rice paper as my pretext, and there received me, in the steam of the pots, a slight señor in an apron who, on seeing my scribe's face — those above recognize our archive face — wiped his hands, made the exact bow of a functionary, and introduced himself with full title: Lord of the Stove of this house, keeper of the domestic record, at your service.

A god of the kitchen. A Zao Jun, of the kind every Chinese house once had its own, as every Roman house had its lar. The guilds of above rhyme across continents too; it is one of the few things that still moves me.

—

His history he told me across years, over visits of chrysanthemum tea, and it was the history my archive was missing: that of the best-organized heaven the species has produced.

Because the heaven he came from was not a pantheon: it was a *government*. A Jade Emperor above, with his court; ministries; hierarchies; gods with rank, with promotions, with removals — a god who failed could be demoted by celestial decree, and a diligent one promoted. And at the base of the whole organization chart, millions of functionaries like him: one stove god per household, charged with watching the family's life through the year and *filing his report* each new year, when the family daubed his image with honey — to sweeten the report, with that theological pragmatism no other faith has matched — and sent him off with firecrackers to the office above.

"I keep my house's record, colleague," he told me the first afternoon, and the "colleague" I earned right there, no paperwork required. "Who was good, who lied, who worked, who wasted rice. Every year I go up and I report. It is a good system. A serious system."

And then I asked him what my archive obliged me to ask, because I knew what had happened in his land: that in 1912 the last emperor of men had abdicated, a child, edict and all; that the state sacrifices at the Temple of Heaven — the liturgy that for millennia knotted the government below to the government above — had ended with the dynasty; that the Mandate of Heaven, the doctrine that legitimized thrones for three thousand years, had been left with no earthly addressee. And that afterward had come the other thing, which in the years he and I drank tea was just beginning: entire campaigns of a new government against "the old," temples closed with hammers, the most systematic attempt since Akhenaten — Akhenaten at industrial scale — to end the whole celestial organization chart by decree.

"And your office?" I asked him. "The throne below empty, the Temple of Heaven a museum, your superiors persecuted. To whom do you file your report, lord of the stove?"

The kitchen god of calle de Dolores poured more tea before answering. Serious bureaucrats answer with the cup full.

"I file it just the same," he said.

"To whom?"

"I don't know." He said it without drama, the way one reports the level of a well; where have I heard that tone, I thought, and it was the tone of all my best dead. "For years now, when I go up at New Year, the office is... not empty. *Silent.* I leave my report. No one stamps it. I come back to my kitchen." He looked at me over the cup. "You are an archivist, colleague: you tell me. Is a record worth less because no one reads it?"

I was carrying a book that had then spent half a century shut in a safe. The question passed so close to the bone that I did not answer, and he, courteous like his entire civil service, did not press. He served me noodles. The functionaries of the stove let no one leave without eating; in that too they rhyme with my Lar.

Another tea afternoon I asked him whether no one, in all those years, had tried to reopen the office. He set down his cup, and for the first time I saw on the functionary the face reserved for delicate files.

"There was a man," he said. "The general who kept the government when the child abdicated. In the third year of the republic — that is how they count them over there; new papers want their own calendar too — at the winter solstice, he went to the Temple of Heaven with the complete rites: he studied them, had them reconstructed from the archives, fasted what is prescribed, dressed as is prescribed, and offered the sacrifice of State as the emperors offered it, so that heaven would confirm him on the throne he was already having built." He poured more tea, exactly. "I was not there; those of my rank do not go to those offices. But the rumor climbed the whole ladder, because we all wanted to know the same thing you want to know right now: whether heaven answered."

"Did it answer?"

"The same as it answers me." And there was no triumph in his voice; serious bureaucrats do not celebrate other people's files. "Silence. The difference, colleague, is what each of us did with the silence. I went back to my kitchen and stayed at my post. He proclaimed himself emperor anyway, paperwork out front and mandate nowhere behind, and the whole country laughed in his face, and the provinces left him, and within months he had to give back the title, and within the year he was dead. Of illness, say the bulletins. Of shame, we say in the guild." He looked at me over the cup. "A rite is not a procedure, scribe. A procedure can be done by anyone who knows the steps; a rite only works if the one above and the one below both sign it. He tried to stamp his own letter of appointment for himself. That is not religion. That is forging documents."

I did the arithmetic of the years in my head, out of professional deformation, and went cold at the result, and I set it down because my archive collects exactly these rhymes: the winter in which that man demanded confirmation of the silent heaven was the winter that opened the year in which I, on the other side of the world, said to my book *no more*. The same year. He forcing a door that no longer opened; I closing one that nobody had asked me to close. Heaven kept silence with us both, and out of the two silences came an empire of eighty-three days and a closure of one hundred and eleven years. Archives rhyme too, and almost never in one's favor.

I offered him inscription, of course. It was my duty, and by then I wanted him in my book with an unprofessional fondness. He heard out the complete laws, nodding with technical approval — "well drafted," he murmured at the third law, and it was the only time I have watched anyone evaluate my sentence as normative text — and at the end declined it with the most bureaucratically perfect reason of my four millennia:

"I cannot sign in a foreign archive without a letter of authorization from my superior, colleague. And my superior does not answer. If he ever answers, the first thing I will ask him is whether I may. Word of a functionary."

"And if he never answers?"

"Then I remain in my post until further notice." He smoothed his apron. "A post is not abandoned because one's superiors keep silence. It is abandoned when you are relieved. No one has relieved me. This family eats hot food every day and someone has to keep the record of that. Heaven will reorganize itself when it reorganizes itself. Kitchens cannot wait for heaven."

—

The apology I owe him is this: that afternoon in 1963, I pitied him. Walking home with my rice paper under my arm, I thought: poor orphaned functionary, filing reports to a dead office, the last employee of a government that no longer exists. I thought it with the arrogance of a woman who carries a great book and seven thousand signatures, and I was wrong about everything, and it took me sixty years to measure the size of the error.

Because I, the one with the great book, got tired. I closed my archive in 1915 and put it in a box, and what that cost has been told in this chronicle. And he did not. He goes on. He goes on today, above calle de Dolores, with another generation of the family — the grandchildren leave him the honey at New Year "for tradition," they say, not knowing that tradition is what the living call the just-in-case. He goes on filing his annual report to a silent office, not missing a year, for more than a century now. And in his land, as far as I know from the newspapers and the travelers, the kitchens filled again with lords of the stove as soon as the decrees got tired, because decrees always tire before kitchens — Amun knew that already, and Serapis knew it, and now it stands on record three times in three empires of my archive: *heaven can be killed in writing, and never, not once in history, has anyone managed to kill the stove in writing.*

The smallest bureaucrat of heaven turned out more faithful than the scribe of all the heavens. That is the record. When I finish these notebooks I am going to climb the stairs on calle de Dolores and let him read it, with the honey I owe him, and if he asks me again whether a record is worth less because no one reads it, this time I will answer.

It is worth more, colleague. Signed: the woman who opened hers again.
