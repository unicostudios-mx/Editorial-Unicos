# 18. The Workshop

This chapter takes place on the Tuesday afternoon of that week — the same Tuesday as the confession, a few hours later — one day before the deva and before the worst night, and I set it here, out of its order, on the advice of Valeria herself, who on reading these notebooks told me: "The reader is going to need to breathe where we breathed." She is right. Between the house of cushions and the door left ajar there was one common ordinary Tuesday, and common ordinary Tuesdays are — I say it with four millennia of data — what the living miss most about the dead.

That Tuesday I taught Valeria to darn paper.

She had come "for an hour, tops" to collate dates, and by the hour's end she was seated on Amalia's bench — I did not tell her whose bench it was; neither did the bench; benches are discreet — in an apron of mine, with her hands in glue water. I set her to doing what every apprentice is set to on the first day, since Sumer: grafts on dead paper. You take a sheet broken past saving, one that is no longer a document but material, and you replace a corner with new fiber, and you repeat it until the hands understand what the head cannot: that paper is not stretched — it is *persuaded*.

Valeria, doctor of history, author of three books, fought her first corner for forty minutes. I watched her sweat. I watched her curse under her breath with a repertoire I hadn't known she had and which I applaud. I watched her, at the end, hold up her sheet with its graft crooked but firm, with the exact face of triumph of all my apprentices of all my centuries, and I understood that what was happening to me was the thing I had forbidden myself for a hundred years: I was teaching the trade again. The last had been Amalia. The one before, a copyist in Antwerp. The thread is long and it is always the same thread.

"This is what you'll do with the Register," she said suddenly, looking at her corner. "The stubs of the torn pages. You're going to graft them like this."

"When this is over, yes."

"Teach me that one too." She didn't look up. "The graft on rag paper. If I'm going to inherit a wounded book, I want to know how to heal it."

I noted the date in my head, where I note what I don't want shifting on me: the Tuesday my witness asked, without knowing it, for her first scribe's tool.

—

By mid-afternoon she had taken down from the shelf, all on her own, the matter I had been dodging for a week: she set about cataloguing the dead pages of the Register, with gloves I forbade her and the wrist-bone technique I taught her, and being Valeria, it was not the great ones that moved her first. It was the odd ones.

"Edna." Her discovery voice, the archive one. "Does this say what I think it says?"

I leaned over. Page four thousand two hundred and something, provincial Greek lettering, a trembling signature.

"Crepitus," I read. "Minor god of the latrines. Swore in Alexandria. I remember him: he came at night, dying of embarrassment, to ask whether my book accepted 'those of his line.' I told him my book accepted anyone whom somebody needed. He sat there crying, roughly where you're sitting now."

"And who prayed to a god of—"

"Everyone, doctor. Everyone, every day, with a devotion most brief and most sincere." I shrugged. "He was not a great god. He was a *necessary* god. He outlasted Apollo, if you want to know. Apollo had hymns; Crepitus had urgencies. In my archive, urgencies yield better returns."

Emboldened, she went on cataloguing odd ones, and found another who deserves his line: a Roman page, stiff-handed, peasant.

"Robigo?" she read.

"The rust of the wheat. The blight." I nodded. "A god whose entire office was *not to come*. Nobody ever asked him for anything, in centuries: they asked him for pure absence. The Romans, who were the sky's best lawyers, held him a festival every spring on his exact day, with a procession out to his grove, so that he would stay home." I shrugged. "He signed my book with one condition no one else has ever set me: that I write down that his work showed when it did not show. He lasted as long as rain-fed wheat lasted in his country, and went out without complaint, like a blight that can no longer find a field. His page is among those I most respect: there are gods of giving, doctor, and there was at least one of refraining. The sky also had officials whose job was keeping their distance, and the mortals paid them the distance's salary punctually. Think about that the next time someone tells you that praying is asking."

Valeria laughed as she had not laughed since the Lar's wake, head thrown back, and the whole workshop loosened by one degree, and I used the laughter to look at her at my leisure, which is a thing we scribes do too little: looking at the living while they are well, taking no notes, only because they are well.

It was she who called the pause. She set down the glove, poured herself coffee from the pot, and asked, in the careful tone she uses for questions she has already chewed on:

"Can I ask you something about the guild? Your guild from up there." She pointed her chin at the Register. "You've got seven thousand pages of gods who signed in order to last. In four thousand years... did no one ever ask you for the opposite? Did nobody ever come and ask you to *tear out* their page?"

The workshop was left with only the street's noise.

"Nobody," I said. "And believe me, they came asking for everything: name changes, page relocations, to be sewn in next to their families. But that — nobody." I sat down on my bench with my coffee. "I thought about it a great deal at the time. I concluded it was the first law of anyone signing anything: nobody enters a contract thinking about the exit door."

"Or there was no door," said Valeria, slowly, "and everyone knew it but you."

And there it is, for whoever afterward studies how investigation is done: the Tuesday before everything, in a borrowed apron with forty minutes of darning behind her, my witness put her finger on the fifth law three days before the killer taught it to us in ink and pins. I did not listen to her with the care she deserved. I was looking at the light of the workshop, at Amalia's bench occupied again, at the crooked graft drying in the press, and I allowed myself — let it be on record, because negligences are also acts of record — one entire hour of pure Tuesday.

I do not regret it. Let that be on record too. Of all the errors of that week, the Tuesday hour is the only one I would commit again without changing a thing.

—

Before leaving, backpack already on her shoulder, Valeria stopped in the doorway and looked over the whole workshop, slowly, as if taking inventory.

"You know what's strange about this place?" she said. "I've been coming here for six years. I always thought it was a fancy stationery shop with pretensions. And it turns out it's..." — she searched for the word with her hands, the way Santa Muerte would search for one days later, though I didn't yet know that — "...it's a chapel. The only chapel in the world where what is venerated is that things *last*."

"It is a workshop," I said. "Nothing is venerated here. Here we work."

"Uh-huh." She settled the backpack, smiled with the Ibarra jaw. "And what do you think venerating is, Edna?"

She left without waiting for an answer, because Valeria's best questions are the ones she leaves to collect on their own. I stayed behind with the dirty brushes and the loaded press, and washed everything slowly, and straightened Amalia's bench just so, and if that night I left the good lamp of the workshop lit a while longer than necessary, that is nobody's business and nevertheless here it stands on record, because I have finally learned what that extra while of light is called, though it took me four thousand years and an atheist in a borrowed apron to teach me.

Just in case. It is called just in case.
