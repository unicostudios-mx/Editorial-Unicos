# 6. The Fire of the Hearth

I came down from the mountain with the census in my bag and my four-thousand-year-old knees informing me that they exist, and reached the workshop past eight on Thursday night. I put water on to boil. I took out clean paper to draw up the visiting list, which is different from the census list: the census says who is alive; the visiting list says in what order I will knock on their doors — and that second list, I want it clear from here on, was mine. My handwriting. My four millennia of judgment.

Hestia went first. First in the sewing, first in the danger. I wrote her name on the top line and stood looking at it while the water came to a boil, and what I did then was not think about the killer, or the pages, or the order.

I began remembering a day with her. A whole day, with the complete sharpness of my sentence, which has not forgiven me a detail since the salt flats. And since that day explains everything she was, and since this book exists so that those who will no longer be on any record may be on this one, I set it down here. On Thursday night it was a memory. Now it is an act of record.

—

September 1985. The nineteenth.

The city's center fell in on it that morning, and I will not recount the earthquake here, because of that there is archive enough and grief enough, and because my book registers those above, and that day belonged entirely to those below: to the ones who dug with their hands, the ones who formed bucket lines, the boys who learned that week what their bodies were for. I will say only that I was in the workshop when it shook, that the workshop held, and that by midday I was out through the Roma and the Juárez looking for my own, who were eight then and are half that now.

I found Hestia at the corner of Marsella and Dinamarca, in a refugee camp four hours old, in front of a building they had gotten all the living out of and were about to stop getting the dead out of.

There was, on the broken sidewalk, a two-burner hotplate connected who knows how to a gas tank someone had dragged from who knows where. And behind the hotplate, in a sweater that wasn't hers, white hair pinned up with a pencil, an old Greek woman was heating café de olla in a laundry tub of sheet metal, and serving it out into plastic cups, into peltre mugs, into washed mayonnaise jars, into whatever people brought. She didn't ask names. She asked: "With sugar?"

I got in line like anyone else. When I reached the hotplate she looked at me one second with those banked-ember eyes of hers and said:

"You take the two big pots. The rescue crews don't eat unless you carry it to where they are."

Four thousand years a scribe, and that day I was a coffee runner. It is one of the best jobs I have ever had. We walked the camps of the district all afternoon and all night, she ahead with the pilot light of her faith carrying her, I behind with the pots, and I watched the goddess of the hearth work the worst day of her new city. I set down how the fire of the hearth works a disaster, because no one else is going to set it down:

She performs no great miracles. She shores up no walls, finds no survivors. She goes from group to group looking, with a portera's trained eye, for whoever has already lost their inside, and in that person's hands she puts something hot. That is all. That is all, and I watched her do it two hundred times that night: the engineer who had gone twelve hours without crying and cried the moment the cup warmed his fingers, because the body understands permission before the head does. The woman who would not let go of a framed photograph, and let it go to take the coffee, and with her hands free let herself be held. Small heat opens. Those above have known it always; those below know it too, they just say it differently: they say *"have you eaten?"*

Around three in the morning we sat down, she and I, on a curb on calle de Berlín, the last coffee in the tub split between us, and I asked her what I had not asked in twenty-eight centuries of knowing her, because we had never had a night so broken and so long:

"Do you miss your great fire? The official one. The one from before."

Hestia blew on her coffee. Adjusted the pencil in her hair. And told me, in her recipe-telling voice, the story I knew from above and had never heard from her mouth.

"In Rome they called me Vesta, you know that. Same fire, different uniform. There they kept me that flame that never went out, in its round temple, with its girls tending it in shifts. Six hundred years, seven hundred, a thousand. The flame of the State." She took a sip. "A pretty flame, I won't say it wasn't. But it was a flame *on payroll*, Edna. It burned because there was a decree that it burn. And one day another decree came saying no more, and the new god's people arrived, and they put it out. Just like that, with proper procedure and everything. The last of my girls I managed to see in her old age; some lady of the new regime tried to take even the collar of her office from her, and my girl told her to keep it, that nobody was taking her memory. Brave, my girl. They were all brave."

"And what did you feel? When they put it out."

I waited for the grief. I had spent centuries assuming Hestia carried a grief the size of an empire, and one assumes a great deal when one does not ask; let this line stand among my errors, for this book already knows I collect them.

"Relief," said the goddess of the hearth.

I sat there with my cup halfway up.

"Relief, child. Because that great flame was my beautiful cage. All the fire of the State gathered in one room, tended by decree, and me tied to it, being *important*." She said "important" the way one names an illness already cured. "And meanwhile, in every house on every street of that empire, there was a little flame nobody tended by decree — a woman tended it because her people needed feeding. That is where I really was. That is where I always was. When they put out the official one I thought: good, then. Now only the true ones are left to me." She pointed with her cup at the camp, the two-burner hotplate, the metal tub. "Have you ever once seen me short of fire?"

Day broke and we went on serving. The building on Marsella got its disaster put into paperwork that year, was repaired slowly, and when it had tenants again it needed a portera, and the best-recommended portera in the area was a little old Greek woman who had poured coffee for half the colonia during the quake. That is how she came to have her building. She did not choose it for the eighteen kitchens. Though, knowing her, I won't swear the contrary either.

—

That is what I remembered on Thursday night, with my visiting list before me and the water boiled and forgotten, as happens to me when memory takes the upper hand.

And now comes the part that costs me, and I write it because this book does not let me choose only what leaves me standing handsomely, and because Valeria, who reads these notebooks as I fill them, told me last week that my restraint was turning into distance. She is right. I hate her a little for being right.

I looked at Hestia's name on the top line of my list, and I decided not to go Friday morning.

I decided it with reasons. There are always reasons; at my age they manufacture themselves. That the Lar was more exposed, with his vecindad of open doors. That Hestia was better approached in the afternoon, when the building empties and we could talk long. That in the morning I had ink to prepare, the book to check, thinking to do.

Underneath the reasons — which, as I said, manufacture themselves — was the truth, and the truth was smaller and uglier: I had just spent an entire night with her in my memory, warm, with coffee, and a part of me — the coward part, the part that closes safes — felt I had already paid the visit. That the remembering counted. Four thousand years of knowing better than anyone in this world that memory feeds no one, that testimony is presence or it is nothing — and that night I served myself the opposite lie, because I was tired, and I still smelled of the mountain's bunchgrass.

I copied the list out clean. I wrote: *Friday afternoon, Hestia.*

I turned off the lamp, went to bed, and slept straight through, dreamless, with the clear conscience of those who have already made their list.

She died Friday at six, with the coffee served in two cups.
