# 10. The Confession

Valeria arrived at the workshop on a Tuesday at eight in the morning, without coffees, which is her way of declaring war, and set on my sewing table a beige folder of the kind she uses for her articles.

"I'm going to tell you a story," she said, "and you're going to tell me where I'm wrong."

For ten years, she told me, she had been collecting impossible case files. Discreetly, the way a serious historian collects: not publishing them, not believing them, noting them in a notebook with the ironic label *Miracles*. The man from Iztapalapa, 2019, whose body tested at nine hundred years old and whose death certificate got lost three times. The woman from Oaxaca whom the forensic team described for one week as "of impossible identity," and by the next week no one on the shift remembered processing her. A pattern of bodies that appeared, scandalized a laboratory, and then went blurring out of the paperwork the way a dream blurs by mid-morning, down to anecdote and then to nothing.

"I always thought the pattern was bureaucratic," she said. "Incompetence looks supernatural if you watch it fondly. But I've spent two weeks reviewing all of it together, and the pattern isn't procedural. It's a pattern of *dissolution*. The files don't get lost, Edna: they evaporate on a schedule, as if reality had a deadline for ceasing to be on record." She opened the folder. "And then there's my favorite restorer."

She began laying photos on the table the way one lays down cards: me at the Incifo, taking the hand of Kulla's body. Me on the sidewalk of Marsella, the night of the eighteen golden windows, questioning the neighbor in the bata. Me entering the vecindad on Mesones at eleven-thirty at night, my bag pressed to my chest.

"You lied to me about the fibers," she said, without triumph, and that was what hurt: she said it with sadness. "Modern cellulose. I took one to the institute's microscope, because I stole it from your glove, because I'm that kind of friend. Rag paper, linen and hemp, gelatin sizing. That paper hasn't been manufactured in centuries, and you knew it better than anyone in this city." She sat down. "A body with Euphrates water in its teeth. A Greek portera who dies the same week with her building lit up like an altar. You, going from one death to the next like someone following a list. And a notebook of mine with ten years of dead people who evaporate. Tell me where I'm wrong, Edna. Because the only story that adds up completely is one my head won't allow me."

I looked at my friend: thirty-eight years old, the best mind I have dealt with in this century, daughter of guadalupanos, an atheist with the ferocity of the daughters of guadalupanos, with the dark circles of two weeks of not sleeping over a pattern. And I thought of Kulla with his page in his hand, and of Hestia serving the second cup, and of the sentence that had walked me home from the Juárez: *my family is dying on me.* The living ones left to me fit on the fingers of one hand.

One does not adopt a mortal lightly. I had forbidden myself since 1915, and that prohibition was also a door I had closed, and by now this story knows what happens with my closed doors.

"You're not wrong about anything," I said. "You're missing data. Do you want the data?"

"I want the data."

"The data has a cost, Valeria. It isn't money and it isn't faith. It's worse: it's that the data is true. You pay once, and there are no refunds. The woman who walks out of this workshop this evening will not be able to go back to being the one who walked in this morning. I tell you this from experience: I have spent four thousand years unable to go back."

Val is brave, but she is not reckless, and I watched her take the price seriously: she was silent a full minute, looking at her folder of miracles. Then she said, very quietly, the most honest thing I have heard from a mortal in a long time:

"If I leave right now — will I ever stop thinking about this?"

"No."

"Then I already paid." She closed the folder. "Give me the data."

—

I went to the back room for the book. I set it on the table, wrapped, and unwrapped it without ceremony, because the ceremony it would provide on its own, and it did: the whole workshop seemed to arrange itself around the Register the way an audience arranges itself.

"Before I tell you anything, you're going to do one thing for me," I said. "You're going to put your hand on this book and ask me whatever you want. Anything. And I will answer with my hand on it."

"Is it a Bible?"

"It is the opposite of a Bible. Scriptures are dictated by those above and written by those below. This one was written by one from below and signed by those above." I pulled her chair closer. "It has a property that will interest you methodologically: upon it, one cannot lie. Not 'must not' — *cannot*. You will have to discover on your own that this is true, because it is exactly the kind of claim your profession obliges you to demolish. That is why we start this way. Ask."

She put her hand on the book's skin fingertips first, the way one touches an iron that may be hot. I watched her register the warmth — that exact warmth of a hand that lets itself be held — and I watched her decide it was my stove, the weather, anything. Good. Let her doubt. Her doubt was the instrument.

"What is your name?" she began, in seminar voice.

"Enheduanna." Hand on the book, tongue obedient. After a century of "Edna Aranda," my full name in my own mouth rang like a bell in a small room. "Daughter of Sargon of Akkad. I was high priestess of the moon at Ur four thousand three hundred years ago, and I have kept this register ever since."

I watched the name strike her. She is a historian: she knew exactly who Enheduanna was. She knew about my calcite disc, my hymns, my signature, the first of the species. Watching her collate the author from her seminars with the restorer who billed her invoices was a spectacle that nearly made me smile in the middle of my own confession.

"That's impossible."

"That word has already been spent by the forensics examiner. Ask verifiable things. You are wasting a precision instrument."

She straightened. And the interrogation I wanted began: dates, dynasties, readings of tablets that only three living Sumerologists could corroborate, traps — she asked me about a hymn that does not exist, and I answered that it does not exist; she misquoted a verse of mine on purpose, and I corrected her with its author's pedantry intact. Two hours. By the second hour she was no longer asking history: she was asking the other thing.

"Do the gods exist?"

"They all existed. Four remain, and one page it is not yet my place to tell."

"Who killed the man in the cement?"

"I don't know." The book warm under our hands, the truth coming out even, without effort. "I know how: they tore his page out of this book. I know it goes in order: the order in which they are sewn. And I know who is next, and that is why since Friday I have been sleeping on a cot on calle de Mesones, next to a two-thousand-year-old gentleman who makes the best atole in this city and won't let me pay for the gas."

Valeria Ibarra, doctor in history of religions, third-generation atheist as she likes to say, sat looking at our two hands together on the skin of the Register. And then she did the thing that makes her who she is, the thing no believer and no unbeliever would have done, the thing only science does when it is truly brave:

"All right," she said. "Working hypothesis: all of this is true." She took out her notebook of miracles, opened it to a fresh page, and wrote the date with an almost steady hand. "Then I need the complete list of your living pages, the interval between the two deaths, and a map. If the killer is going in order, Edna, your book isn't just the motive."

She looked up, and her eyes were no longer the morning's eyes. The cost was paid; what she had bought was this: fear with a method.

"It's the list of victims. And you've just told me you sleep directly on top of the next one."
