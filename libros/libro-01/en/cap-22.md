# 22. Val's Mother

The Friday after the Lar's death, Valeria kidnapped me.

I say it with technical precision: she arrived at the workshop at one, took the brush out of my hand, said "today you don't eat alone," and loaded me into her car admitting no appeals. I had spent two days with the killer's burnt fragment in a restorer's pouch, staring at it the way one stares at a sleeping snake, and she had spent two days watching me stare at it. Mortals with a method know when a witness needs to eat caldo.

"We're going to my mom's," she said at the first stoplight. "On Fridays she makes mole de olla. Fair warning: there's an altar, there are veladoras, there's a Virgin. If that bothers you for... guild reasons, say so now."

"Valeria," I said, "I have eaten in the temples of twenty religions. The only house where I don't eat is the one where they don't serve me."

"Perfect. One more thing." Light turned green; she didn't move; she looked at me. "My mom knows nothing and will know nothing. To her you're Edna, my friend the restorer. But I warn you, my mom *sees* things. Not things from above: things about people. Seventy years reading faces over a quesadilla stand. She's worse than your book."

Her name was Socorro. She lived in Portales, in one of those houses that grew room by room across the decades, with bougainvillea on the roof and a zaguán that smelled of epazote from the sidewalk. She received me with a full-body hug, without knowing me, because there are señoras in this city who hug first and find out later, and she held me squeezed for three seconds, and on releasing me looked at me with her daughter's same eyes but without the method, which is to say: with the pure blade.

"Ay, hija," she said — to me, who am four thousand two hundred years her senior. "You carry dead people."

"Mamá," said Valeria.

"What? It's a manner of speaking." It was not a manner of speaking. "Come in, come in, it's getting cold."

—

The altar was where they always are: in the best corner of the sala, the Virgen of Tepeyac at center, two veladoras, market flowers well arranged, and the photos. A mustached man in an eighties suit. A boy in a school uniform who never reached any other uniforms. Doña Socorro caught me looking and did not let me ask with courtesies:

"My husband and my oldest boy. Rodolfo and Beto." She said it setting plates, without lowering her voice, the way one says the names one says daily. "The señor from a heart attack, twenty years ago. The boy, a motorcycle, thirty-two years ago. Go on, sit down."

We ate. The mole de olla was the kind treaties should be signed over. Doña Socorro talked about the market, about the gas man's neighbor, about how Valeria worked too much and brought her neither grandchildren nor dogs, "at least a dog, Valeria," and Valeria rolled her eyes and I laughed more in one hour than in the whole month, and at no moment, not one, did the altar behind me stop burning. The houses of this city are like that: the conversation at the table and the veladoras behind, each in its turn, no one in anyone's way.

At coffee, doña Socorro got up to change the water for the altar's flowers, and she did it the way I have watched true gestures done all my life: without interrupting what she was saying, with hands wise from repetition. And Valeria, watching her, asked the question. Not of me. Of her mother. In a voice I didn't know on her: the daughter's voice, not the doctor's.

"Mamá, were you never angry?" She pointed her chin at the altar, the photos, the Virgin. "When papá. When Beto. You prayed to her every day, and every day you... Didn't you ever feel you were talking to nobody? That when you needed her most she didn't help you?"

The room went the size of the question. I sat very still with my coffee, because I recognized what I was hearing: it was Isis's question, the five-hundred-year one, the one they asked everyone in my book in every language — *why didn't you defend us?* — asked now from below upward, in a sala in Portales, with mole de olla on the table.

Doña Socorro finished arranging her flowers. She dried her hands on her apron. And she answered looking at the flowers' water, not at us:

"How could I not have been angry, criatura. When Beto, I stopped speaking to her for two years. Two years, just like you hear it, not one vela did I light her; ask your aunts. I said things to her that you don't say to anyone, standing right here where I'm standing now." She crossed herself small, pro forma, for the things said. "And then I came back."

"Why did you come back?" Valeria, very low. "If she didn't answer you. If she didn't give him back."

"Because I realized one thing, and I realized it all by myself, which is how the things that are any use get realized." She turned around at last, with her blade eyes. "I didn't pray to her so that they wouldn't die on me, Valeria. That I understood late. I prayed to her to *bear the world where they die*. Those are different prayers, hija. The first one nobody ever granted me, not me and not one of the women who sell alongside me, not anyone since the world began, and the day you accept that, you get very angry — two years or twenty. And the second prayer..." — she shrugged, with the simplicity of expensive conclusions — "the second one she has granted me every single day. Here I am. I make mole. I scold my daughter. I change the water for my flowers. You think one bears that alone?"

She glanced at her Virgin a second, sideways, the way one glances at a business partner.

"I don't know if she hears me, criatura. At this point that's not even what I discuss. I know that I hear her. That's what I work with."

—

We left with the evening coming down. Doña Socorro lets no one go empty-handed — in that she is a colleague of every doorman and hearth in my book, though she will never know it — so I came away with a topper of mole, a bundle of tortillas wrapped in a checked napkin, and instructions to return the topper in person, "so you'll have an excuse to come back, because my daughter's friends don't last her: she works them too hard." In the zaguán, while Valeria went for the car, she held me back a moment, hand on my arm, and inspected my face with the blade eyes, with no disguise at all, the way one inspects a stew.

"You're carrying less now," she ruled. "And let the record show it wasn't the mole, eh? Mole fixes the body. What you had got fixed in the talking." She gave my arm two pats, the kind given by señoras who have buried and cooked in comparable quantities. "You listen well, hija. It shows right away: you listen with your whole body, like people who have sat many vigils. I'll just charge you with one thing: women who listen that well always forget it's also their turn to talk. Don't wait until the end to tell what's yours, because by then the caldo doesn't stretch."

And she closed the zaguán on me with an everyday goodbye smile, not knowing — or knowing; with the señoras of this city I have never been able to be sure, and I have spent four millennia in the trade of being sure — that she had just given the scribe of the gods, in two minutes and with a topper in her hand, the instruction this entire notebook is obeying.

—

Valeria drove back silent down the Eje Central, and I let her, because her silence was working and working silences are not to be interrupted. It was at Niño Perdido that she spoke:

"You know the mechanics." Eyes on the traffic. "You know *exactly* why those above defend no one: you explained it to me with laws and with sewing. The broken faith, the broken arm, the spiral. The entire engineering of abandonment." She swallowed. "And my mom knows none of that, and she is fifty years ahead of your archive. How?"

I took my time answering because I wanted to answer well, and this notebook inherits the complete answer, which is the closest I have come in four millennia to a theology of my own:

"Because your mother understood the one thing my archive cannot register, Valeria. I can document for you why the sky is silent: it was poverty, not indifference; I have it on record; I have the bodies. But that explanation only serves those above. Those below are served by the other half, the half your mother made alone in two years of lighting no velas: that prayer is not a transaction to be granted or denied. It is a company to be accepted or not accepted. The gods of my book died because their people asked them for the first prayer, the impossible one, and they broke from not being able to grant it. And your mother survived because she learned to pray the second one." I watched the city go by, the millions of lights coming on. "I have spent this whole story saying that to witness is a form of love. Your mother corrected me today without knowing me: it is also a form of enduring. Put it in your notebook, doctor. It will be the best footnote of your career."

Valeria said nothing. But at the next red light she took out the notebook, the one of the actas, and wrote, and I looked away, toward the street, to let her set down in peace what her mother and four millennia had just signed together.
