# 33. The Last Page

I gave Valeria access to the complete Register that week, because after Māra's visit there was no secret left worth a risk, and because in four thousand years no one — from above or below — had ever looked at my book with the kind of attention she has: the attention of one who loves the truth more than her own comfort. I set her a table beside mine. I taught her to turn pages with the bone of the wrist, without finger acid. I left her alone with seven thousand years of dead, and went off to sew a missal, and the workshop sounded the way it should always have sounded: two trades breathing together.

On the third day I heard her stop.

There are reading silences and finding silences, and a bookbinder tells them apart from the other table. I looked up. Valeria was at the end of the book, past the living pages, past the empty centuries I left sewn in, in case the sky took to giving surprises again, at the last gathering, the one that borders the back board. Her two hands hung still in the air, the way one's hands stop when instinct already knows and the head not yet.

"Edna," she said. "Why is there an entry on the last page?"

"Because the book was made complete," I said, not setting down the missal, because there is news that can only be given while sewing. "Every well-made book is sewn with its colophon."

"Edna." Her voice, very level. "The handwriting isn't yours. I have seen your hand in four alphabets and six thousand years and this is not yours. And the name..."

"The name is mine."

I set down the missal. I went to her table. And I looked, for the first time in one hundred and eleven years — I avoided it; one learns to leaf through one's own book with narrowed eyes, the way one passes quickly in front of certain mirrors — at entry seven thousand two hundred and four.

I did not write it. It was there the night of the salt flats, when the book dawned made: the last page, and on it, in a hand that belongs to no hand I have ever seen write — a hand like the edge of a hot knife on wax, which is as close as I can say it — my full name. *Enheduanna, daughter of Sargon, priestess of Nanna at Ur.* And below, nothing: the blank space of an open entry, waiting for its final line the way they all wait.

"Explain it to me," said Val, with the ferocious calm that comes out of her when she is afraid. "Explain to me why your name is inscribed in the register of the gods."

"Because that was always the second law, read in full." I sat down beside her. Great things are said sitting down. "'You will not die while the book is open.' I understood, at my fifty mortal years, that the book protected me. It took me centuries to read the mechanics: the book does not protect me, Valeria. The book *contains* me. The night it was made, I was sewn in. I am the last entry. I live by being inscribed, exactly as they do: the book is my last believer, just as it was Kulla's. The only difference is one of order: I am at the end of the sewing." I laid my hand flat on my own name, and the page, warm, acknowledged me the way the whole book acknowledges me: without drama, the way one hand acknowledges the other. "When I closed in 1915, the candle began to burn down page by page. Eleven, Kulla. One hundred four, Hestia. One hundred fifty-nine, the Lar. The line advances. And at the end of the line, when all the living pages are closed and the book is *finished*, the flame reaches the last entry."

Val sat looking at my name. I watched her do the arithmetic I had spent weeks not saying aloud, and watched her finish it, and watched her get up from the chair and walk to the window and stand there, back turned, a long while, because there are sums that cannot be finished while looking at people.

"That's why you're going to 'finish the book,'" she said at last, to the window. "You said it in those words. You said it to him in those words. The deva's vigil, Tláloc's farewell, writing the deaths as they happen. It isn't just the well-done grief, or the responsibility, or the trade. Finishing the book is your death, Edna. You are organizing your own line and you put yourself at the end of it."

"Yes."

"And when were you planning to tell me?"

"Today," I said, and it was true, and the book was right there for anyone who wished to verify. "I left you alone with the book knowing what you would find, because I am a coward in a very old and very specific way: I know how to deliver every piece of news except my own."

She turned around. Her eyes were bright and she had the Ibarra jaw on, which is a jaw that does not negotiate.

"No." One syllable, the same size as my "no more" of 1915, and I thought that the doors of this story all open and close on two syllables. "No, Edna. There is another option and you know it: the open book stops the line. You did it, it worked, the candle stopped. We can... I don't know, dose it. Open for periods. Search the laws for a loophole — with you inside there's cause, you are not a frozen dying thing, you are *well*, you—"

"Valeria."

"—four thousand years and you want me to stand here watching you go out over a guilt you already paid, is that the well-done grief? is that what you're going to teach your dead?—"

"Valeria." I took both her hands, the way she took mine the day of the cement, with the photos. "Look at me and ask me the question with method. The one you would ask any witness in this case. The only one that matters."

It cost her. Method, when you truly love someone, is the first thing that falls and the last thing recovered. I watched her pick it up off the floor, piece by piece, with an effort I will not forget while I have ink left.

"How old are you?" she asked.

"Four thousand three hundred."

"Are you tired?"

"No." And the answer surprised me more than her, because in 1915 it would have been yes, and the book never let me say it then because no one asked me upon it. "Tired, I was. Now I am *finishing*. They are different states, Val. Tiredness wants things to stop; finishing wants things to close. I have spent a month accompanying deaths the way they ought to be accompanied and it is the first time in forty centuries that my trade and I are at peace. Do not take my peace away to give me time. Time I was already given, all of it, and I have seen what it does in industrial quantities: nothing. Time only does good work by the day's wage, in lives of human size, where each day is worth something because it ends."

"And what falls to me?" It broke on the last word, and she did not pick it up, and rightly so: there are breaks that are the voice telling the truth with the body. "To watch? To note in my notebook how my best friend dies? Is that my part in your finished book?"

And there it was, my last pending record, the one I had been postponing with more art than any other, and I signed it:

"No. Your part is mine." I went for the Register; I put it in her hands; it weighed on her what it weighs: everything. "Someone has to keep the book, Valeria. Finished, closed, complete: seven thousand two hundred and four entries and one blank page. Someone who knows what it is, who will neither pray to it nor burn it, who will *read* it. The gods had four thousand years of a scribe. What they were — the telling, which is how the dead of above survive — is going to need a historian." I closed her fingers over the linen. "You asked me for your part: witness me. To the end of the book, with your notebook and your jaw and your gas pimienta. And afterward, tell us. All of us. The way true things nobody can believe are told: with rigor, with sources, and without fear of being laughed at."

Valeria Ibarra looked at the book in her hands. She looked at her impossible friend, four millennia standing in a workshop that smells of rabbit-skin glue. And she said what her people say, the method people, when they accept what has no remedy and decide to do it well, which is the highest form of faith I know and the only one I have never been able to inscribe for anyone:

"I'm going to need full access to the archive," she said, her voice still wet. "And you are going to answer all my questions, in order, however long it takes. All of them, Edna. Starting from Ur."

"Starting from Ur," I promised.

And so it was signed, with no book between us, because there are oaths stronger than my sewing: the ones made between mortals. I, for the purposes of that table, was already one of them. I have never received a higher promotion.
