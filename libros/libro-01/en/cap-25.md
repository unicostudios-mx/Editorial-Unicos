# 25. The Ones Who Crossed the Sea

From the Register. Havana, 1847. Written with permission, and with washed hands.

I begin this chapter differently from the others because its story is not mine to tell in full. The lords I speak of here are alive — alive today, growing today, their houses full today — and one does not write chronicle of the living without permission. I asked for it. The answer was what one would expect of them, and I have put it at the end, because it is the best line in the chapter and I did not write it. Here goes only what I hold on record from one afternoon in 1847, told with the care with which one touches another's altar.

—

I was crossing from Europe to Veracruz, with a long layover in Havana, and in the port — ports have always been my best archive; everything the world moves passes over their docks, including what is above — I felt what my trade had not felt in centuries: divine presence that was *new*. Not a survivor from my book hidden inside a custom. Something with the force of the ancient days, deep and wide, living on the far side of the bay.

I crossed on the little Regla ferry, among baskets and day laborers, toward the town of the hermitage, and I did not have to search. On the shore, watching the water with her hands on her hips, stood a large señora dressed in blue and white, and the sea of the bay moved around her the way dogs move around their person: with no need of orders.

I knew before whom I stood, because knowing is my trade, though her name was not in my book: the mother of the waters. The lady her people brought from the far side of the other sea.

I presented myself with the formulas. She looked me up and down, exactly as long as necessary, and said:

"The one with the book of the dead from over there." It was not a question; mothers do not ask what they already know. "I felt you come off the ship. You carry heavy, hija. Sit down."

We sat on the seawall of Regla, facing the hermitage of the Virgin that watches the bay, and there, that afternoon, the lady of the sea told me what she chose to tell me — not one finger's width more — of her people's crossing. This notebook reproduces the essentials, which are, besides, in the archives of men, because this history the enslaved wrote with their memory and your historians have been documenting letter by letter: how hundreds of thousands of people torn from their lands crossed the ocean in the holds of the slave ships; how they could carry nothing — not an image, not a drum, not a seed; and how, in that absolute nothing, their lords crossed anyway.

"The ones in your book travel in things, don't they?" she said to me. "In statues, in bundles, in scrolls. Mine had no things. Mine were carried across *sung*." She touched her temple. "Here. In the song and in the rhythm and in the name said low. Every one of those ships, scribe, with everything it was, carried more heaven in its holds than the cathedrals up on deck. And the masters' gods above never knew, because it never occurred to the masters that a god could travel steerage."

She told me — this too your colonial archives hold, with their seals and their ink, for history sometimes keeps the proofs of what it did not understand — about the cabildos: the brotherhoods "de nación" that the masters' own law permitted, so that the enslaved and the free of each homeland could gather on Sundays with their drums, under the advocation of some saint of the church. There, she said, in those courtyards-with-permission, the fine work of three centuries was done: each lord of her people learned to live *inside* the saint who lent the door, the way the hand lives inside the glove without ceasing to be hand. The lord of thunder and fire, inside a saint of sword and tower. She herself, the mother of the sea, inside the dark Virgin of that hermitage at our backs, the one who watches the bay, patroness of those who work the water.

"Does it offend you?" I asked her, nodding at the hermitage, because it was my guild's question. "Living under another's name."

"Does it offend you that they call you Edna?" she answered, and laughed with her whole chest, and the water of the bay laughed with her in little waves. "Hija, names are doors, they are not houses. We always knew it; yours died of not knowing it. The house is the rhythm, the house is the song, the house is the daughter teaching her daughter. That, not even the ship could take from us. The rest is signage."

At the end, as was my duty, I opened the Register on my knees and offered inscription: to her, to hers, to the entire heaven that had crossed the sea sung. I explained the laws, the third above all, the guaranteed testimony, the page as last believer.

The lady of the sea heard me out in full, with the courtesy of those who have presided over councils. Then she laid her hand a moment on my book — the Register kept still and respectful, the way a swell keeps still alongside good docks — and declined it with a sentence I leave here as it was said, because it is among the three or four largest things anyone has said to me in four millennia:

"Keep your pages for the ones who are afraid of being forgotten, hija. We already died once, out there on the big water, and look: here we are, with a new house and the children growing. Whoever has already crossed that water signs no papers of eternity. Eternity is touched on Sundays."

—

That was 1847, and my book holds not one page of theirs, and it is the most honorable absence in my archive.

What follows I do not hold on record: the world does. A hundred and eighty years later, the lords who crossed sung are more alive than nearly everything I ever inscribed: their houses multiply through this continent and the others, their children cook for them, their drums sound from Havana to the neighborhoods of this city where I write. Of all the roads of survival this chronicle registers — the move, the disguise, the merit of one's own, the telling — theirs is the only one that needed neither me nor anyone of my guild, and that is why this chapter is short and goes with washed hands: there are histories a scribe should only point to, with respect, saying *there*. There stands a house the worst ship in history could not sink. Their full chronicle belongs to them, and the day they choose to tell it, there will be no shortage of hands better than mine.

I only attest to the afternoon on the seawall, and to this: that on taking her leave, the mother of the waters straightened my rebozo with two fingers, the way one straightens the collar of a daughter heading into bad weather, and said:

"And you, stop carrying so tight, scribe — whoever carries tight drowns with canoe and all. The dead float better when you loosen your hold on them a little. Ask the sea, which knows more about that than your book."

I have spent a hundred and eighty years asking it. The señora was right: it knows more.
