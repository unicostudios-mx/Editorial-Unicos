# 38. The New Books

The last jobs of a workshop are done the same as all the others. That is the dignity of the trades and their secret: there are no final gestures, there is the same gesture, made for the last time with the same care. I learned it from a god who leveled a course of brick before letting himself be killed, and I applied it that week, which was the most laborious and the most serene of my century.

The first job was the final restoration of the Register.

I took it apart completely for the first time in four hundred years. Every gathering checked, every seam gone over with new hand-waxed thread, the leather fed, the corners consolidated, the stubs of the three torn pages — Kulla, Hestia, the Lar — grafted with rag paper I made myself in the courtyard vat, as in the sixteenth century. Not to disguise the wound: so the wound would stop tearing further. There is a difference, and my whole life fits inside it.

On the three grafts, in good iron-gall ink, I wrote the records that were missing. Because I realized, sewing, that I had been left with three deaths unwritten — the only three of my whole sentence I never registered — and that leaving them as gaps was granting the last word to someone else. I wrote them as they should be written, with date, with cause, with one line of who each of them was. On Kulla's I put his Guanajuato prophecy, fulfilled in reverse: *he said he would lay the last brick of my tomb; I was the one who laid the last paper of his.* On Hestia's, the eighteen stoves. On the Lar's, a single word under the date, his own, the word of his oath, because doormen need no speeches: *custodio*.

The second job was the new folio volume.

I made it from nothing, with the best hands I have left: rag paper from my vat, sewn on true cords, boards covered in pale leather — pale, yes; registers are not obliged to inherit mourning — and not one word inside. Three hundred blank pages, of the common ordinary blank of all paper that waits. No law sewn in, no sentence, no scribe tied to the spine. A free book, which is the most dangerous and most generous thing my trade knows how to make.

Santa Muerte came for her answer one Sunday at noon, without an appointment, as is her right as a new neighbor. She arrived with the denim jacket and a chiquihuite of tamales de dulce, "because I didn't know what you bring to a workshop," and that sentence won me over whole before she sat down.

I sat her where they all sat. I served her coffee. And I told her no.

"Your register is not going to be mine, muchacha." I told her looking at her straight on, because the important nos are said that way. "And I want you to understand the whys, all of them, because a no without whys is a closed door, and of those I no longer give.

"My book is finished. Not empty: finished. It is the work of a world that closed its cycle, and it holds inside its laws, its debts, and its dead woman, who is me. To inscribe you would be to hang your new name from a sewing that has already given all it had: it would pass down to you my laws without asking you, my going-out without your owing it, and a borrowed scribe who already has a date. The gods of this land carried the contracts of a foreign heaven once already, and I watched how it went for them. Not with you. Not at my table."

I pushed the new folio across the table, into her hands.

"This is yours. And it belongs to the ones who will come with you, because they will come: this people is not done manufacturing sky — it comes out without their trying; it is their finest craft. A new book for the new gods, without one inherited law. The laws you all will write, and I hope you write better ones: fewer oaths and more recipes, if you ask me — but nobody asks me, and that is how it should be."

The youngest goddess in the world laid her hands on the blank book the way I have seen hands laid on sacred things: both of them, from underneath, weighing it. She opened it. She turned the empty pages slowly, and across her muchacha's face passed something ancient, because she may be young as a cult but she is old as a trade, the oldest after mine: she also keeps a register, in her way, and she knows what a new one weighs.

"And the scribe?" she asked, at last, the only thing there was to ask. "A book like this needs a hand. Who is going to write us?"

"The trade finds hands. It always finds them: I was an exile on a salt flat." I shrugged, with a freedom I had not known I had. "Look for her without hurry. You will have centuries and candidates to spare. I ask only two things, colleague to colleague, and they are advice, not laws: that she swear with witnesses, whatever she swears. And that the book have an exit door. Mine had none, and look what it cost to close it."

"An exit door," she repeated, storing it away with that care of hers of one who forgets nothing because in the end everyone is on her record. "I'll put it on the first page, scribe. Before any name."

She rose, the folio against her chest, the way one carries a healthy child. At the door she paused, and for the second time made me the bow from above to below, and this time she held it a moment longer, and said, with the deep murmur of many people praying low in a large room:

"When it's your turn, don't hurry and don't say goodbye to me. With me there's no need. I will be there on duty." And with a muchacha's smile, the last one I saw from her: "You're going to like how I work. I've been told I take after you."

—

The third job was the handing-over, and it was the hardest, because it was not a job of hands.

I put the Register into Valeria's arms one Wednesday afternoon, the April sun coming in orange through the half-lowered shutter, and dictated to her, with no book between us, the terms I had spent a week polishing:

"It is yours from today, not from afterward: I want to watch you carry it while I can still correct your posture. With it go my working notebooks, the four thousand three hundred years we have put in order these months, and the whole workshop, which is already in your name; I owe it to the guild to leave it with someone who knows that books are not resurrected — they are kept company so they last. You will find the two benches even, by the way: the clients' one no longer wobbles. We fixed it together, as promised, and in this guild the small promises are kept first, because they are the ones that hold up the large ones.

"And now the instruction, Valeria. The only one. Hear it complete before you fight me, because I know you: *publish it as fiction*."

She fought, of course. With all her guild's reason: that it was the most important document in human history; that the truth deserved the status of truth; that she had the rigor, the sources, the career to defend it before any academic tribunal on the planet; that publishing it as a novel was burying it on the new-releases table between vampires and life coaches.

I let her finish. Good arguments deserve their full space, above all when they are going to lose.

"All of that is true," I told her, "and all of that is exactly the problem. Think as a historian one moment longer: what does the world do with the truths it cannot bear? It reviews them. It assembles committees for them, carbon-fourteen, expert findings, debates, denials; it turns testimony into case file and case file into scandal and scandal into oblivion. I have watched truth die of excessive defense more times than of attack, Valeria. You saw it yourself at the Incifo: the impossible papers evaporate on a schedule. You want to give the world the Register? Don't give it to the experts. Give it to the readers.

"Because fiction, doctor, is the only archive humanity does not audit. Nobody asks a myth for its birth certificate: they learn it by heart. The gods of my book survived twice: first on faith, then on the telling. Mythology is how the dead of above survive; the grandmother's story is how the dead of below survive. It is the same mechanism and it is the best-proven one there is: four thousand years of warranty, and I am the warranty. Publish it as a novel. The ones who need to know it is true will know. It is the oldest property of true stories: they do not convince — they *are on record*. One feels them being on record."

Valeria looked at the book in her arms. She looked at it a long time, all her objections stepping aside on their own, the way the sky steps aside when a king's elephant comes down.

"And the blank page?" she asked, no longer fighting, already an editor. "What do I do with it in a novel?"

"You print it blank. As is. In every edition, forever." I smiled at her, and felt the smile come from far away, from the salt flats, from a night at Nicaea, from a hill to the north with its candles. "Let every reader pause a moment before it, with nothing to read. The ones who don't understand will turn the page, and nothing happens. And the ones who understand will do exactly what everyone has done before that leaf for seventeen centuries, your scribe included."

"What?"

"Stand still a moment. Feel a kind weight. And go on reading without being able to affirm a thing." I settled the linen over the book — gesture number one million, the last. "That, doctor, is the closest thing to the truth that can be printed."
