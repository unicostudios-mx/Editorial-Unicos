# 19. Nalanda

From the Register. India, eight hundred years ago.

I came late to India, like all the Western world, and with the same prejudices as anyone arriving from established guilds: I believed I was coming to inscribe one more pantheon. I spent two centuries understanding that there was no such pantheon but a deeper accounting, and that for the first time in three thousand years the problem was not that the gods didn't want to sign my book.

The problem was that my book, there, was redundant.

An old monk at Nalanda explained it to me, the first time I passed through, with a kindness that still burns:

"Your register writes down which gods live and which die, yes?" He smiled with his gums. "Here the doctrine has been writing that down for fifteen hundred years, madam. They all die. The gods too. The gods above all: the higher the heaven, the longer the oil and the surer the lamp of going out. Your book is a census; ours is the law. Why count sheep when you already know they all sleep?"

Nalanda. How do I tell it to you, who know it by its bare foundations and its red bricks lined up for tourists. It was the largest university in the world and there was no second: monasteries nine stories high, ten thousand students, masters who came from Korea, from China, from Java. And the library — three buildings; the tallest was called "the one that touches the clouds" — with hundreds of thousands of manuscripts: the whole doctrine, and with the doctrine, the most exact description ever written of the fauna above: the heavens by stories, their inhabitants, the spans of their lives in figures that gave you vertigo, and the five signs of their death, set down with the coolness of a nursing manual.

The devas did not worship that library. They *frequented* it. It was what nothing else in the world ever was for those above: a mirror with a steady hand. They came down at night — I saw them; the librarians did too, and noted it in their margins with complete naturalness — to read themselves.

That is why, when it burned, they all came down.

—

It was the end of your twelfth century. The kingdoms of the north were falling one after another to the Turkic horsemen, and a war party under a captain named Bakhtiyar Khalji — the Persian chroniclers set it down with pride; the Tibetan ones, with mourning; both agree on the essentials — fell upon Bihar. Nalanda they took the way one takes a warehouse. The Persian chronicle records the conquerors' own perplexity: they searched for the treasure and found books; they asked what the books contained, and by the time they found someone who could explain, they had already killed the ones who knew how to read them.

The library burned for months. That is not a figure of speech of mine: it is the tradition you yourselves preserve, and I confirm it with these eyes. Hundreds of thousands of palm-leaf manuscripts burn slowly, with a sweetish smoke that is not forgotten, and that winter the sky over Bihar had a second permanent dusk, low, at the level of the broken rooftops.

I arrived on the eighth day, the book against my chest, along roads full of fleeing monks carrying in bundles what could be saved: each bundle, a splinter of the library. And on the first night, camped in a burnt orchard within sight of the towers, I watched the devas come down.

They did not come down as in their paintings, in glory and mandorla. They came down the way decent people come to a fire: quiet, in plain clothes with the light badly hidden, keeping to the edges so as not to be in the way. Hundreds. The heavens, story by story, emptying themselves of the curious and the bereaved over a university in embers. Not one intervened. They could not, or it was not their place, or both: the fire belonged to the history of men, and those above, by then, had learned by force of hunger what was theirs to do and what was not.

They watched their mirror burn. I have watched that way. I know what was burning for them: not information — the doctrine survived; there were copies, there was memory, there was Tibet — but the *place*. The one site in the world where they were set down exactly. One can live without being described; the poor know it, and the small gods. But to lose the only good description of you that existed is a second death brought forward, and the devas, who do not die of the hunger for faith, turned out to be mortal from that.

Śakra found me. I did not seek him out; one does not send a message up to the king of the Thirty-Three. He sat down in my burnt orchard, the size of a pilgrim, the glow of the fire on his face, and stayed silent the time a large ember lasts. Then he said:

"You are the one with the census of the dependents."

"I am."

"I have come from seeing the third building. The roof has fallen." He looked at his hands. "We have perfect memory, scribe. I can recite you the stories of the heavens and the years of my dead without missing a syllable. And yet, when the roof fell, all of us watching felt the same thing, and none of us had the word. A dying librarian lent it to us, right there, on the esplanade; he said: 'there is no longer anywhere to verify us.'" He raised his eyes. "I want to be on record in the one that remains. Show me your book."

I showed him everything: the laws, the sewing, the closed pages, opaque and soft, of the ones I had been losing. He read the way good kings read: slowly, and skipping none of the dead. When he reached the blank page he stopped his finger a millimeter short, without touching it, and looked at me with a question he did not ask, and I thanked him for not asking it, and he understood the thanks. The two oldest guilds in the world are discretion and memory, and that night they camped together.

He swore upon the warm ash, with the formula he himself asked to compose: not the formula of gods who ask to be sustained, but another, his own, which stands on his page and which I copy with his eternal permission: *I am on record. I ask nothing more.*

—

It was as I was putting the book away that I saw him.

At the edge of the orchard, where the road began, a man stood watching the fire. He wore traveling clothes with no dust on them. I did not know how long he had been there: he was one of those presences that do not arrive, but that one discovers were already there. The devas in the orchard had seen him too, and they did not leave, but they *composed themselves*, the way people in a parlor compose themselves when someone enters with whom everyone has history.

He came over without haste. Up close he was a man of middle age, agreeable, with the kindly tiredness of those who help out at other people's mournings. He looked at my wrapped book, then at the fire, then at me, and greeted me with a small bow, correct, guild to guild.

"Scribe of the census," he said. His voice was the most rested thing I have ever heard: a voice without one urgency in any century. "A great night for the archives."

"Who gives the greeting?" I asked, with the old formula, my hand on the book.

"One of the trade." He smiled slightly, watching the towers. "Look at that. Months, it will take. The men believe they are burning doctrine, the devas believe they are losing their mirror, the monks believe they are saving splinters. And they are all right, and none of that is what is happening. What is happening is simpler: the composite decomposes. That is the only news, scribe, and it has been the only news since before your book. Everything else is ways of taking a long time to read it."

"My book exists in order to take a long time," I said. "That is its function."

"I know." He looked at me full on then, and in his agreeable eyes there was no bottom — not like an abyss: like a clear sky, which is worse. "And I respect it, believe me. You write down what ends, so that ending will not be the same as never having been. It is fine work. I do the other shift of the same workshop: I am what ends, reminding what thinks itself eternal that the contract has fine print." He settled his traveling blanket, ready to go on his way, and said it without any threat, the way colleagues remark on the weather, which is why I have not forgotten it in eight hundred years: "You and I do the same job, scribe. I don't keep a book."

He went off up the north road, toward the glow. The devas watched him go without hatred and without relief, with that taut courtesy old families keep for certain relatives.

"Who was that?" I asked Śakra, though my body had already told me.

The king of the Thirty-Three took his time answering. He was watching the fire, and his face, newly sworn into my book, had the expression of one who has reviewed a very long account and found it, against all hope, correct.

"The one who disputes," he said. "He circled the Awakened One for years, raising objections. He lost every one." He rose, brushing off the ash. "That is what frightens me most about him, scribe, and I tell you for your census: he lost every argument, and he is still the one among us all who is least wrong."
