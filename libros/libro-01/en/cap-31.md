# 31. The Pact

He came on a Thursday, at closing time, when the street settles into that streetlamp amber that forgives everything. I was lowering the shutter and a hand stopped it from outside, without force, with the courtesy of someone holding a door at a wake.

"Still open, scribe?"

I knew who it was before I saw him, by my body: my body went calm. That was the datum. Faced with danger, the oldest flesh in the world bristles like anyone's; faced with him, nothing. The body filed him at once in the category he belongs to, the one that has no remedy and therefore no alarm: the rain, the night, the downhill slope.

"I'm open," I said, and raised the shutter, and let him in, because I had been expecting him for weeks and because the inevitable, my guild knows it well, is to be received: one does not also give it the satisfaction of being right about the scene.

Up close, in the workshop light, he was the man from Nalanda without one day added: middle-aged, plain clothes without dust, the kindly tiredness of those who help at other people's mournings. He looked over my shelves with genuine interest, the way people of the trade look at workshops of the trade.

"Nice place," he said. "It smells of lasting."

"It smells of rabbit-skin glue. What can I do for you, Māra?"

He smiled faintly at hearing his name, without surprise, and I pointed him to the clients' chair, and he sat down the way those who have waited long sit: without spending the moment. On the table, between us, lay the Register wrapped in its linen. He did not look at it longer than politeness allows. Politeness, in him, was exactly the problem.

"I've come to make you a work proposal," he said. "But you are a scribe: you will want the record in order first. Ask. I'll put myself wherever you say."

And he himself extended his open hand toward the book, offering himself to the oath, with the naturalness of one who knows the instrument better than its owner.

I unwrapped the linen. He laid his palm on the skin of the Register — the book did not stir; I note it because I expected anything but that indifference: my book, which trembles before a blank page, received the lord of impermanence the way one receives the mailman — and I laid mine beside it.

"Did you kill Kulla, Hestia, and the Lar?"

"I tore out their pages," he said. The truth came out of him as it comes out of me: flat, without effort. "Their pages were the last thing holding them. In the sense that matters to you: yes. All three received me, they knew who I was, and not one asked me not to. That does not absolve me; I offer it as a datum, not a defense. The brick one asked me for five minutes to finish leveling a course. I gave them to him. He worked well."

Four thousand years of interrogations, and no answer had ever hurt me so cleanly.

"Why?"

"Because I have spent a century looking at what you refused to look at." There was no reproach in the voice; there was something worse: patience. "You closed your book and went off to restore missals. I stayed with them, scribe. It is my trade: I am the one who stays to the end, at all the endings, since before your book. And what the closing set in motion I watched up close, decade by decade: the hearth fire forgetting the names of tenants it had kept warm for years, and weeping with shame in its portería; the lar checking his bolts forty times a night because he could no longer remember whether he had locked; the brick one, at the end, standing in front of the works unable to recall what comes first, the wall or the trench." He made an exact pause. "You sewed them to your book. Your book was their last meal. You took it away. I am only serving the glass of water one gives to the one who will not eat again. In my country that is not called killing. It is called compassion with a calendar."

"It is called ending a life without consent."

"Consent." He repeated the word, weighing it like a museum piece. "Ask the book, here we both are: did any of the three close his door to me? Did any cry out, run, call you — you, who slept six nights three meters from the lar's cot?" He waited. The book, warm, kept silent with the two of us inside. "They did not call you because they were ready, scribe — all of them except you. The long decline had done for them the work that good old age does for mortals: it had taken their leave of everything. The formality remained. I do formalities."

"And you leave them notes fixed with a pin. Addressed to me." I leaned toward him. "That is not a formality, Māra. That is correspondence. A being without urgencies inventing a hurry and signing it. What do you really want from me?"

And then, for the first time, the lord of impermanence took his time answering. He looked at our hands together on the book, his and mine, the same picture this workshop had seen with Valeria, with what a different war. And when he spoke, the kindly voice had underneath it his full age, which is not an age: it is the age of everything else.

"I want you to say it," he said, and he addressed me as tú for the first time in eight hundred years, and the tú was the message. "With your hand where you have it. One sentence, scribe. That closing was a choice. Not a tiredness, not an accident, not a grief that overflowed you: a choice, yours, with consequences that others paid. I have spent a century hearing you say 'I got tired,' 'I didn't know,' 'the laws they never told me.'" He leaned in now, and the whole workshop seemed to list by one degree. "I lost every argument with the Awakened One — did they tell you? Every one. Except one that was not an argument: I asked him to let go of life, and he let it go when he chose, not when I said. There I learned the one thing I still lacked: there is no victory over one who owns his act. There is only victory over one who tells himself stories. All of you, above and below, tell yourselves the same one: that things ran out on you, and not that you let them run out. You are the memory of both worlds. If you say it, hand on your book, it is said for everyone. That is all I want. Say it, and I stop."

And there was the pact, entire, served like Hestia's second cup:

"Say it," he went on, gently, "and the pages that remain, I do not touch. You write the deaths, at their own pace, as the deva asked you: hand laid on, line by line. You accompany them all to their water, without me, without hurry, without pins. You finish your book with dignity, which is what we both know you came to this century to do. Only you finish it *having told the truth*. That seems to me a scribe's price."

The worst of Māra is not that he argues well. It is that he does not lie and does not need to: he offers you your own truth, with interest. Because he was right about the fact: I closed by choosing. That April afternoon, with the disgust and the two syllables: I chose. This whole story had been my way of coming to know it. And yet, with my hand on my book and his hand beside it, I also understood why I was not going to give it to him, and I told him the way one dictates a record, slowly, so that it would stand:

"It was a choice." His face did not celebrate; he was listening, and that confirmed what I already knew: the sentence by itself was not the victory; the victory was the sentence *delivered to him*. "I made it badly, alone and without witnesses, and I have paid for it in the only coin I have, which is knowing it. But you have the wrong record, Māra. You want me to sign that choosing to close is the same as choosing their deaths. It is not. Between my closed door and your pin there is a space, narrow, exactly the width of one person, and in that space fits everything you lack: turning back fits. Opening late fits. Asking forgiveness with your hands full of work fits. Mortals do it every day, with fewer centuries and less guilt than mine. It is called answering for what one did, and it will never be signed over to you, because answering is the opposite of your trade: you close accounts; I carry them."

We sat looking at each other over the book, the lord of all endings and the lady of all records, and in the workshop not even the dust moved.

"I am going to finish the book," I said. "I am going to write their deaths as they happen, because they asked me — not you. I am going to accompany them to their water. And every line will say what it was: that they went out through a door I closed, and that they left in peace by a hand I held out. Both things. That is my record, Māra. Not your way. Mine."

Māra withdrew his hand from the Register with the care with which one lifts a flower off a coffin. He stood, smoothed his dustless clothes, and at the door, the half-lowered shutter cutting his figure against the amber of the street, he turned with the first and only expression I have not learned to read on him in eight hundred years. It might have been respect. It might have been pity. In him, I have suspected ever since, they are the same thing looked at from two sides.

"You will write the first death with your own hand," he said, "and halfway down the line you will understand that it was my offer under another name. That day, do not come find me to tell me. I will be there."

And he went off down the street, unhurried, the only being in the universe who has never needed hurry, leaving in my right hand — the writing one — a cold that was not his.

It was mine. Māra's diagnoses are always one's own. That is what makes him serious.

—

Valeria arrived twenty minutes later, with a bag of tacos de canasta she had bought "on instinct" at the corner, and found me sitting on the clients' bench, which is not my bench, with my hands on my knees.

She did not ask what had happened. She set the tacos on the table, moved the Register aside with the bone of her wrist, as I taught her, poured the two coffees, and we ate without talking, she on her bench and I on the clients', the workshop smelling of old paper and potato guisado, which is a combination no incense of my four millennia has improved on.

"You know what's the strangest thing about these last weeks?" she said at the end, wiping her fingers with a napkin. "That nothing seems strange to me anymore. This morning I catalogued the signature of a god from three thousand B.C., in the afternoon my mom sent me mole in a tupper, and now I'm here having tacos with you right after you turned down a pact with the impermanence of the universe. And my head already files it all in the same drawer: Tuesday."

"Welcome to the guild," I told her. "That is what lasting feels like."

She stayed that night too, on the sillón, the workshop keys already on her key ring — I don't remember when I gave them to her; I remember that it cost me nothing, and everything costs me. And before I turned off the good lamp there came, from the sillón, her method voice with the day's last question:

"Edna. When this is over... the workshop's going to need two good benches, no? The clients' one wobbles."

I turned off the lamp so she would not see my face. There are questions that are plans to stay, and they move us scribes in direct proportion to how little we were expecting them.

"It's wobbled since 1943," I said in the dark. "Tomorrow we'll fix it."
