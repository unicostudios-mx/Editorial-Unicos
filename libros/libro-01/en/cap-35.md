# 35. The Last Rain

The old fire I went to sit with in the small hours, eleven days after the deva, because the book woke me.

It was that simple and that enormous: at four twenty in the morning, the warmth of the Register, which sleeps with me since this story began, changed temperature at one point, the way a bed changes when someone on the far side is leaving. I laid my hand on it without turning on the light. The page with the ember signature, the only one in the book. It was cooling.

I reached the San Cosme market at five, when the diableros are unloading and the aisles smell of crushed cilantro and freshly lit bulbs. Doña Chela's food stand, third aisle, is one of those that open before all the rest because they carry the cargadores on credit and on faith. And in her sheet-metal comal, in the corner where the fire has spent forty years never quite going out — "this comal doesn't go cold, marchanta, it's one of the old ones," she boasts, not knowing what she boasts of — lived what remained of the lord of the old fire, the wrinkled one, who was ancient already when the peoples of the corn came to the valley.

He barely spoke anymore. For years Huehuetéotl had been more ember than voice: a heat with memory, a settling of the coals whenever someone said something kind nearby. I sat on the stand's bench, ordered a cinnamon tea I was not going to drink, and laid the book open on my knees, under my apron, pen ready.

"You've come," said the ember, in the old language of the valley, with a sputter so slight doña Chela took it for grease. "Punctual scribe. Like the water, you."

"How do you want to go, abuelo?" I asked him, softly, in his language.

"Like this." The coals settled. "With people about to have breakfast. It's what I always was, hija: the fire in the middle. Not the lightning's and not the sun's. The one that warms the first tortilla. — A silence with amber inside it. — Write it short. We old ones have letters to spare."

I wrote it short. His final entry is three lines, and since he can no longer boast of them and doña Chela will never know she was the last priestess of a god of forty centuries, I copy them here, one single time, so it may be known how fire dies when it dies well:

*Dawn in San Cosme. Doña Chela's comal warms the first tortilla of the day.*

*The old fire watches it brown, as ten thousand years ago, as if it were the first.*

*He goes out with the tortilla ready. Nobody's breakfast was cold.*

The page closed on its own, warm, smelling one second of copal though there was no copal. Doña Chela, at that exact moment, blew on the comal, tested it with wet fingers, and said, puzzled, to no one: "I do believe this comal is finally going to go cold on me." I bought tamales from her and gave them away at the metro entrance. The funerals of the household gods are like that: they fit in nobody's basket.

—

And then only the water remained, and the water summons the way it does everything: by its own means.

That same afternoon the sky over the south turned a worked gray, a construction-site gray, and over the Ajusco there came down one single curtain of rain, straight and narrow as a finger set on a map. Valeria, who by now lives half in my workshop and half at her university, watched it from the window and said, without irony, notebook in hand: "They're paging you. Answer."

We went up the next day, the two of us. I asked her to: the book says my trade is the witness, but this story had taught me the correction the book does not carry: the witness needs a witness too. At the clearing trailhead a young man of the water people was waiting for us, serious, with two wooden staffs, and he asked nothing: "The señor says come up easy, there's time. He says the time is his to set."

In the clearing of petitions they were all there. The usual nine and many more: water people from the towns of the volcanoes, mayoras and mayores with their crosses dressed in ribbons, families with flowers, pots, bread. A long altar at the foot of the big oak, with cempasúchil out of season — cempasúchil knows; the flowers of this land know — and that organization without orders of fiestas that have been rehearsed for centuries. Valeria stopped at the clearing's edge and I watched her understand all at once, with a historian's vertigo, what she was seeing: the last rain-petition ceremony of the last god of the water, celebrated by the only clergy in the world that never once stopped working.

Except it was not a petition. It was the reverse. This time the one bringing the offering was he.

Tláloc stood at the center, hatless, in his good cape, and for the first time in five centuries I saw him *large*: not in size — he was still the short, solid señor — but in presence, the way a father grows large on the day he divides the inheritance. He went from mayora to mayora, granicero to granicero, one by one, a long while with each. I did not hear what he said to them; it was not for me. I saw the effects: men of seventy crying upright, without lowering their heads; a mayora who took the señor's hands and kissed them, and he let her, and then kissed her hands in turn — which no god of my book ever did with any mortal in forty centuries.

Then he came to me. The whole clearing arranged itself without moving, like the orchard at Nalanda.

"Scribe." The original smell, deeper than ever, of earth that already knows. "Did you find it out?"

"I found out everything, lord of the water. Who tears. Why he hurries. Who opened the door." I did not lower my eyes. "I opened it. And I no longer come to shore anything up, or to ask forgiveness with empty hands. I come to what falls to me: to write while, if you will have it, and to stay. Staying is all I bring."

"It is the only thing that can be brought to a rain," he said. "The rest the sky provides."

And he told me, in weather words, short ones, his decision, which was a cycle administrator's and a grandfather's, both: that he could last still — decades, maybe a century, each year more expensive — but that he had done his water arithmetic, which is the only serious arithmetic there is. That his people already knew how to work the seasons without him: "I taught them five hundred years, scribe. The one who teaches well ends up surplus, and that is the only success there is." That the airs and the hills were provided for, with names and responsibilities, because water is not inherited in bulk but in offices. And that a god of the cycle is forbidden, by his own theology, the perpetual hanging-on: "I am the one who goes and returns. If I stay hanging from the going, I break my own word. Water that is not released goes bad, and I am not going to go bad in front of my people."

"When?" I asked, pen already uncapped.

He looked at the sky, which was gray with work, loaded, ready, waiting for him the way a crew waits for the maestro.

"Now," he said. "Before I say goodbye too much. Long farewells are for the dry season."

And the lord of the water turned toward his people, raised his hand the way one waves and the way one blesses — the same gesture; it was always the same gesture — and it began to rain.

It was not a storm. I want that clear in this record, because the endings of the great are always imagined with thunder, and his was the opposite: it was a small rain, local, even, the kind no forecast carries and the grandmothers call blessed. It fell on the clearing and on the towns of the volcanoes and on no other part of the world. And while it fell, the short señor in the good cape went thinning in a way the eyes accepted without understanding, the way one accepts that a curtain of water has no edge: he was *distributing himself*. Into the earth going dark with wetness. Into the pots on the altar, which filled. Into the lifted faces of his people, who received the rain the way one receives a hand on one's head. I wrote while, under the water, covering the book with my body as I covered tablets through three millennia of weathers, and the good ink did not run, because he was in that rain and he has never in his life blotted a line for anyone.

His page closed with the oak's last dripping, and remained, like all of them, opaque and soft. But it is the only one in the book that smells. Still. Of wet earth, faintly, if you know where to put your nose. Valeria says it is suggestion. Valeria says it with her nose on the page, every time she catalogues, one full minute.

The water people did not leave the clearing until nightfall. They were no longer crying: they shared out the altar bread, planned the year's dates, argued offices, presented a months-old baby to the senior mayora, "so the hill will know him." The payroll going on living after the boss: his true monument. Before we went down, the mayora who kissed his hands sought me out, measured me with those eyes the lightning touched fifty years ago, and said:

"You're the one who writes things down." It was not a question. "Then write this down, señora: he did not leave us. He went on *ahead*. Water always goes down to the valley before one does. We'll meet it there again."

I write it down, mayora. In your grammar, which is better than mine.

We came down in the dark. In the colectivo, soaked, the book warm between the two of us, Valeria did the arithmetic in a low voice, because somebody had to do it:

"That's it, isn't it? There's no one left."

"There is one page left that is not touched," I said. "And there is the scribe."

And through the window, over the valley, the city was turning on its millions of lights, which is its way of praying without knowing it, and somewhere down there a polite señor, in no hurry now, was setting out the chairs for the last wake.
