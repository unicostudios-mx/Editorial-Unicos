# 8. The Second Death

I met Hestia the day she swore and we were never apart longer than a century again. That, in my accounting, is intimacy.

She swore in a village of a Greece that did not yet know it would be Greece, and she did it without ceremony, in her way: she sat me down by her fire, fed me lentils with bay leaf, and when I had finished she said "now yes, bring your book," because under her law nobody signs anything on an empty stomach. She was the goddess of the hearth: not of the house — the house belongs to the lares and to the walls, each guild its own — but of the warm center of the house. Of the exact point where fire stops being a blaze and becomes a kitchen.

She was never great. She was *first*. In her country, every offering began with a libation for her, even her fat brothers' offerings, and I never once saw her collect on that precedence. When the hunger came for everyone, hiding cost her nothing: the great ones had to learn to be small, but she had always been the size of a cooking flame. She crossed the centuries stove to stove, brazier to anafre, and arrived in this city in the luggage of a Greek merchant family, in 1911, the way her kind arrive: without anyone bringing her.

For the last forty years she was Doña Estia — like that, the name barely faded; the old gods no longer spend money on disguises — portera of a building on calle Marsella, colonia Juárez. Six floors, eighteen apartments, half of them rented by people living alone. She had keys to all of them, with permission and without it, and her final theology fit in one gesture: when she knew a tenant was coming home from a bad day, a hospital, a divorce, she went up ahead of him and left the stove lit, one low flame, so the house would receive him with its heart burning. No one knew it was her. They all said "strange, I could have sworn I turned it off." All of them, without exception, stood a moment by the flame before turning it off, and that moment was her food. She ate little. As always.

I should have gone to see her Thursday, before climbing the mountain. I went Friday.

Write it down like that, without softeners, because that is how I carry it: I went Friday.

—

I knew from the corner of Marsella and Dinamarca, because the building had all its windows golden. The eighteen kitchens lit at once, at seven in the evening, flames high, and the neighbors beginning to gather on the sidewalk with that herd unease people get when they smell gas where there is none.

I found her in the portería, in her wicker armchair, beside her two-burner hotplate where there was café de olla still warm. Her hands were in her lap, one over the other, and on her hands, folded in four with a neatness I already knew from a morgue sheet, her page.

I unfolded it right there. My Greek hand of the eighth century before you, the oath formula, the mark of her flame. The paper opaque now, soft now, skin of a slackened drum. Page closed, lady closed.

I crouched in front of the armchair, holding the cold hands of the woman who fed me before she would let me work, for twenty-eight centuries, and for the first time since 1915 I did not think like a scribe. I thought, with a simplicity that frightened me: *my family is dying on me.*

Then I thought like a scribe again, which is my way of continuing to breathe, and I registered:

No violence on the body. No forcing of the door. The coffee served in two cups, one across from the other, and the second cup *used*. Hestia gave coffee. To her killer. She gave him coffee, because never in twenty-eight centuries did she let anyone come to her fire without putting something warm in his hands, and the killer drank it.

And the eighteen stoves. That was not his. That was hers: she knew him for what he was. She felt coming what was coming — her kind always know when a fire is about to go out; it is their trade — and she spent what she had left climbing floor by floor, key by key, leaving her lonely ones the house with its heart lit one last time. She did not run. She did not hide. She set out the coffee and waited, hands in her lap.

The people of the building decided that night that Doña Estia had died of her age, which was clearly an enormous age, and that the business with the stoves had been "a last kindness of the señora," and they were right about everything without knowing how much. The whole eighteen apartments came to the velorio, I was told later, and there was café de olla, and no one remembered who had put it on. Good. Let her last banquet be long.

I did not stay for the velorio. I was on the sidewalk, the folded page in my pocket against the book, when I asked the question that had to be asked, of the neighbor from number one, a señora in a flowered bata who had been first out when the windows went gold:

"Did you see anyone leave the portería this afternoon?"

"A gentleman," she said, without hesitating. "Around six. Very polite. He held the door for me."

"What did he look like?"

And here the señora in the bata did something I have watched witnesses do in every century: she searched her memory and found the polished hollow of an erased name.

"Well... normal. Big, no. Small, no either. Like..." — she pulled her bata tighter — "like those gentlemen who go to velorios even when they hardly knew the deceased, you know the kind? Who arrive early, greet everyone properly, give no trouble, and help set up the chairs. He had that air. I even thought: how odd, nobody's died here."

"Did you see his face?"

"Yes," she said, growing distressed by degrees. "I did see it. It's just that it didn't... it didn't stay with me. Can you believe it? And me such a good judge of faces."

I told her not to worry, that it happens, and it is true that it happens: I have spent four millennia watching it happen. The tracks of certain walkers are like the gods' — they come undone without fresh testimony. But the gods of my book leave a body, and this walker left something else: he left *manners*. He drinks the coffee he is served. He folds the page in four and lays it on the dead woman's hands, the way one lays down a missal. He holds the door. He arrives early, greets properly, gives no trouble.

Someone who attends deaths the way one attends velorios: out of respect.

That night I walked from the Juárez to the Centro, to calle de Mesones, no taxi, nothing, because I needed the city in the soles of my feet in order to think. Two deaths. In the exact order of the book: page eleven, Kulla; page one hundred four, Hestia. The next in the line of my living pages slept in a vecindad twenty minutes away, on a cot beside the zaguán, with an eternal veladora whose glass he changed every nine days.

The Lar. The last doorman of Rome.

I planted myself before the vecindad on Mesones at eleven at night and knocked on the great door the way one knocks when one comes from the past: two raps, a pause, one.

Inside, the shuffle of immortal chanclas.

"Who?" said the voice that had spent two thousand years asking exactly that in exactly that intonation, because asking *who* is the entire theology of a threshold.

"The scribe."

Silence. Then the bolts, one, two, three — he does believe in bolts; they are his liturgy — and the door opened a hand's width, and there appeared that face wrinkled like an old walnut, those bitter-almond eyes that watched all of Rome come in and go out through the doors he kept.

He looked me up and down. He looked at the empty street behind me, professionally.

"One hundred and eleven years," he said, just like the other one, because the gods keep better accounts of my absences than of their own hungers. "And you come at night, with the book on you and two closed pages on your face."

"I've come to look after you," I said.

The last lar of Rome gave a short dry laugh, the laugh of a doorman who has heard everything.

"Scribe," he said, opening the door to let me pass: "I am the one who looks after. Come in. There's atole."

I went in. Inside it smelled of veladora and corn. He served me atole in a peltre jarro, set a bench for me next to his cot, crossed himself before his own veladora with an ecumenical shamelessness I had not known in him, and while I burned my fingers on the jarro he said, no laughter left, looking at the zaguán:

"Two in one week, in your order. I know how to count, eh? I was the payroll of an empire." He blew on his atole. "The next one is me."
