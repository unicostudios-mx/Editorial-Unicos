# 39. The Last Entry

This book you are reading I wrote in eleven weeks, in the nights of everything I have told, in the blue-covered notebook Amalia gave me in 1903 and which I had never broken in because I found no story that deserved it. I broke it in for this one. It is my first work in the first person since the hymns of Ur, and it will be the last, and it gives me an old laugh to think that my whole career fits between two confessions: a priestess's to her goddess, and a scribe's to her readers. Four thousand years to change addressees. Nobody can accuse me of rushing.

Last night I set the final period on chapter thirty-eight. I reread straight through, corrected only what was just — modesty wanted to cut more; I did not let it: this book owes itself to the law of my other book, and here too there is no lying — and left the notebook on Valeria's table with a note of two words she will recognize, because they are the two syllables with which the great things of this story close, used at last right side up: *Ya está.*

Today is the last job. Today I write entry seven thousand two hundred and four.

—

We prepared it the way what matters is prepared: without solemnity and with everything ready. Valeria arrived at midday with bread from the Ideal and her new notebook — the old one, the miracles one, she archived yesterday with the label corrected: it no longer says *Miracles*; it says *Records*. She put on music for a while, a low danzón, because she said the silence beforehand was hospital silence and nobody in this workshop was going to die in the hospital manner. She is right about everything lately. She is insufferable and she is my heir; the two things are owed to each other.

The Register lies open at the last page. My name has waited for me there since the salt flats, in that hot-knife-on-wax hand that belongs to no hand I have ever seen, and below it, the blank space of an open entry. I have looked at this space with fear for one hundred and eleven years and with disgust for a century. Today I look at it with a guild eye and find it has a good margin, good proportion, room to spare for what needs saying. Whoever sewed me in here knew about pages. I grant it at this late hour without rancor: the book was a well-bound sentence. I have restored worse sentences.

The pen is the deva's; it belongs to the date. The ink I ground yesterday. The hand is this one, the right, the writing one, the one of four thousand years of other people's records, and it trembles slightly, and I let it tremble: the perfect pulse has already given this book everything it had to give. The last line has permission to resemble life.

I write my death in the present, as a king asked me to. As I owe it to all those who did not have it while I slept. I copy the entry here as I set it down in the Register, line by line, because this blue notebook and that book end up being the same book, and there is no longer any reason to keep them apart:

*Evening falls in the workshop. It smells of rabbit-skin glue, of rag paper, of coffee freshly made. The heir works at her table, near, and her pen and mine sound like two trades breathing together.*

*Enheduanna, daughter of Sargon, priestess of Nanna at Ur, scribe of the Register, dies today of being complete. Nobody killed her: she finished, which is different, and it took her forty-three centuries to learn the difference.*

*She leaves a finished book. Finished, not empty: inside remain seven thousand two hundred and three names that were true, three grafts with their records brought current, one page that is not touched, and this entry. Everything said. The unsaid, on its leaf, blank, as the unknown ought to be filed.*

*She declares, as author, with the authority the book acknowledges in her: the work is whole.*

I felt it obey. Whoever has followed this story knows by now how my book obeys: not like an object — like an agreement. As I wrote the word *whole*, the entire Register went warm at once, from the first tablet to the last stub, seven thousand years of pages coming into heat the way a house comes into heat when at last every one of its stoves is lit, and along the spine, in the sewing, something loosened with a sigh of thread only a bookbinder could hear, and I heard it: the knot of the second law, letting go. *You will not die while the book is open.* The book is no longer open or closed. It is finished. Finished works hold no one: they release, they thank, and they let themselves be read.

Now my hand grows sweetly heavy, the way arms grow heavy at the end of a good workshop day. It is arriving. It rises the way tides rise, and sleep. It does not hurt. I note that it does not hurt, for those who have that datum pending: it feels very much like being, at last, out of pending matters.

On the sidewalk, on the other side of the half-lowered shutter, two presences are waiting without hurrying anyone. I feel them the way one feels that the rain has stopped and that it will rain: one is a polite señor who arrived early, greeted properly, and gives no trouble, keeping the appointment he gave me at this very door: *we will see each other at the last page.* Here we are, colleague. You will grant that the page was worth the wait. The other is a muchacha in a denim jacket who came on duty, as she promised, with her new book under her arm, and has not come in because she is waiting for this to be what it is: a matter for mortals first.

And inside is Valeria, who has already left her table, who is already here, at my side, her hand on my hand the way all my dead taught me it is done: the hand laid on, the line running, no one looking away. She is telling me something with a wet voice and a firm jaw, and I register it as is, because it is the best last sentence any scribe has ever received from her witness:

"Write in peace. I'll stay till the period."

Outside, over the city that was a lake, it is beginning to rain. It is a small rain, even, off the forecast, the kind the grandmothers call blessed, and I know whose it is, because a short señor who does the water's arithmetic better than anyone left it sown for me: the first rain of the season, punctual at my window. It smells of wet earth. It smells of the original.

I have strength left for one line and I have been saving it all my life without knowing. It is for you, the ones on the other side of the page, who manufacture gods without meaning to and die without a record and light the stoves of the sad: I was watching you the whole time.
