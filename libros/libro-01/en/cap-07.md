# 7. Great Pan Is Dead

From the Register. The Ionian Sea, one thousand nine hundred years ago.

The story is in Plutarch and you can go read it; the books of men have carried it down to you with a fidelity that moves me. A merchant ship was sailing from Greece to Italy. Passing near the island of Paxos, the evening still and the passengers awake, an enormous voice called from the land to the pilot, an Egyptian named Thamus, three times by his name, and gave him a charge: "When you come abreast of Palodes, announce that great Pan is dead." And Thamus, with the sea calm off Palodes, shouted the words toward the coast, and from the coast there answered a vast lamentation, of many voices that could not be seen, as of an astonished multitude. The matter reached the ears of the emperor Tiberius, who summoned the pilot, questioned him, and believed him.

So says the text. All of it is true. I was on that ship.

What the text does not say is why I was aboard, and to tell that I have to go back to the spring of that year, when the oldest god of Greece sent for me to commission his death.

—

Pan was not one of the great ones. He was one of the *prior* ones, which is another category: the gods who were already there when the Olympians arrived, the way the mountain is there when the temple comes. He had no clergy worth the name, and no temples but grottoes. His food was never hymns: it was that shiver without cause that takes the traveler at noon in the hills, when the sun is high, the cicadas go silent all at once, and the silence becomes a presence with an opinion. You still use the word: panic. Know that you are quoting a dead man.

That is why hunger reached him before anyone, and by a road no one had foreseen: the roads. Rome did not kill Pan with edicts. It killed him with engineering. Every causeway across a mountain, every bridge, every posting station with its garrison, was a piece of noon that stopped being frightening. The empire made the world passable, and Pan lived exactly on what was impassable. He was the first god I watched die not of oblivion but of *safety*.

He sent for me with a shepherd — his messengers were always shepherds, to the very end — and I climbed into Arcadia to find him, where there were still corners without a paved road. He was in a grotto smelling of goat and wild thyme, small now, slow now, his hooves worn like a poor man's shoes.

"Scribe," he said. "I have watched you cross my mountains for nine hundred years and never once gave you the fright. Professional to professional — such things are respected."

"You gave it to me twice," I said. "Near Tegea, and in Thessaly."

"Three times," he said, with a remnant of pride. "In Thessaly it was twice, but the second one you blamed on the wine."

Then he was quiet a long while, listening to his mountain, and I waited, because one waits for the dying and for gods, and he was both.

"I am running out," he said at last, without drama, the way a man reports the level of a well. "I don't complain of the how. I got the wide world and I walked all of it. But I have watched my own die, the prior ones, one by one, and they all went the same way: alone, in silence, with no one outside ever knowing. The nymph of a stream dries up and not even the stream notices." He raised his head and looked at me with those rectangular goat's eyes I never fully learned to read. "I don't want that, scribe. I was the fear of noon. Let my death be announced at a shout, over the water, in the voice of any mortal at all. Let the unbelievers hear it. Let the shiver, for once, reach them by the news and not by me."

"Why any mortal at all?"

"Because if a god shouts it, it's theology. If a ship's pilot shouts it, it's *news*. The news, Enheduanna, is the only genre men never entirely stop believing."

We discussed the details the way one discusses a will, because that is what it was. He chose the strait at Paxos, waters of his own country, with a good echo. I asked who would give the voice from the coast, who would lay the charge on the pilot. And here this book obliges me to exactness: I do not know. Pan smiled with his old man's teeth and said "that I'll see to, or it will be seen to," and would say no more, and one does not press the dying. I have thought about that voice for one thousand nine hundred years. It was enormous and it was not his. I leave the gap where it is. I have already said that is my trade.

He died at the start of summer, in his grotto, with me present. It was the first divine death I witnessed *in person*, beginning to end, in two thousand three hundred years of keeping the book — until then I had always arrived after, to close pages already cold. It went like this: the smell of thyme grew stronger, then simpler, until it was only the smell of a plant; the silence of the grotto lost its opinion and became a lack of noise; and the body remained, small, ancient, the face in a peace that so resembled tiredness that I have never since been able to tell them apart. His page closed itself under my hand, opaque and soft.

And I noted something that evening that would take two thousand years to become urgent to me. I copy it: *He died better than all the prior ones. The difference was not the pain nor the time. The difference was that someone was watching.*

—

The ship I arranged myself, in the slow manner of those days: I found out which vessel would make the run with a good passenger load — witnesses: living tongues were needed — and bought my place as a widow returning to Italy. The pilot was Thamus, an Egyptian, a serious man, the kind who checks every knot twice. I said not one word to him. That was the bargain: the news had to take him clean, unwarned, so that his fear would be true. Men's faith can smell stagecraft; genuine fear, on the other hand, is contagious to the marrow.

When the voice called from Paxos — three times, *Thamus, Thamus, Thamus*, and the sea flat as a table — I was at the rail with the rest of the passengers, and though I knew what was coming, the hair on my arms rose anyway, because the voice was the size of a coastline and it was placing an order with the simplicity with which one orders bread. I watched Thamus hesitate off Palodes; your text says that too and it is true: he decided he would only shout if there was a calm, and there was a calm such as I have rarely seen, the sea holding its breath. He shouted. He had a good working voice, made for commanding over wind.

"Great Pan is dead."

And the coast answered.

I have heard multitudes lament across four millennia: falling cities, plagues, stadiums. None like that one. It came from everywhere and nowhere, from the crags and the pines and the water itself, many voices that were not voices weeping in astonishment, as if the land itself had just been told. The passengers wept without knowing why, gripping the rail. The man beside me, an oil merchant who had been snoring since Corfu, clutched my arm and asked: "Who is Pan?" In that order: first the weeping, then the question. The order of human things has never again seemed so exact to me.

Who wept on that coast? I note the hypotheses in order of probability, as my archive years taught me: the prior ones who remained, seeing off the greatest of their kind. Or Pan's small creatures, his nymphs and his echoes, orphaning themselves out loud. Or something simpler and larger that I do not know how to name. The gap stays where it is.

What I do know is what my book felt. I carried it against my chest, wrapped, and at the moment of the lamentation it went warm as a hand, warmer than ever before, and I understood a law that was not among my three and that no god had taught me, perhaps because none of them knew it: *grief also feeds.* It arrives late, it saves no one, but it feeds something. That invisible multitude was serving Pan the last banquet of his history, laid after death, and in some pantry of the world that meal was kept.

When the matter reached Rome and Tiberius summoned Thamus, I arranged to be near — palaces always need someone to restore documents; some things have not changed in twenty centuries. The emperor, Plutarch says, questioned the pilot and believed him. It is true, and I add the detail the text does not carry: Tiberius, the most powerful man in the world, old and somber and sick with distrust, heard the whole account without interrupting, and at the end did not ask "is it true?" He asked his scholars: "What did he die of?"

No one could answer him.

I knew, and I kept silent, and lowered my eyes, because the only man in the room who had asked the correct question was the one who administered the roads.
