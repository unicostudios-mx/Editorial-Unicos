# 1. The God in the Cement

There are three ways to kill a book: water, fire, and oblivion. Against the first two there is my trade. Against the third there is nothing, though I spent four thousand years trying.

That morning I was killing oblivion retail, which is all a workshop allows: a Puebla missal from 1789 with its spine in ruins, sewn by someone who hated missals or hands, or both. I work slowly. Not from old age — old age and I have an arrangement — but because haste is the dampness of the soul: you don't notice it until everything has rippled.

Valeria arrived at a quarter past ten with two coffees and that way she has of knocking as if the doorbell had personally offended her. I have known her six years, which in my accounting is nothing and in hers is an old friendship. She is a historian of religions, which means she studies with rigor the things she refuses to believe in. I respect both halves.

"Edna," she said, and set the coffees down on the sewing table, where nothing gets set down. "I need your eyes."

"My eyes are busy with a missal."

"The missal has been dead two hundred years. This is from this morning."

I moved the coffees off the sewing table. Then I held out my hand.

She gave me her phone with the photos open. The first thing I saw was the cement: a fresh pour at a construction site, with the texture of the cheap concrete they sell nowadays to this sinking city. The second thing was the body.

It was inside the pour, up to the chest, as if the cement had caught it sleeping. A small man, of a smallness no longer manufactured: short torso, broad hands, skin the exact color of fired clay. Eyes closed. The face at peace, if peace could be tired.

I swiped to the next photo and stopped hearing the workshop.

Around the body, the cement had not set the way cement sets. It had curdled into bricks. Perfect bricks, laid in courses, with the careful bond of the walls nobody raises anymore, each one bearing a mark on its upper face. The phone's camera recorded it as a defect, a little shadow. I had watched it being stamped into fresh clay when the world was another age: the seal of the master builders of Ur.

"The forensic examiner says it's impossible," Valeria went on, in the tone of a woman who collects impossibles in order to debunk them. "The pour was yesterday at seven in the evening. Nobody saw anything. And there's more: they ran preliminary tests on the body right there, because the judge wouldn't come. Want to know the age of the tissue?"

"No."

"Four thousand years, Edna. Four thousand, with a margin of error that's a joke. A fresh body with Bronze Age carbon. The lab tech wanted to resign."

I gave the phone back. I noticed I did it with both hands, the way sacred things are returned, and she didn't notice, and that was good.

"Why bring this to me?"

"Because two years ago I brought you a facsimile of the Boturini Codex and you corrected three glyphs the INAH had wrong. Because you know things that aren't in the archives and you never tell me where from." She sat down without being invited, which is her way of inviting herself. "And because of the bricks, Edna. You restore paper, but I once watched you look at a wall in the Centro as if it owed you money. What is that mark?"

"A builder's seal," I said. The truth is a muscle; mine doesn't atrophy, for reasons she didn't know. "Mesopotamian. Ur, probably. Third millennium before our era."

"And what is it doing in a concrete pour in colonia Doctores?"

"That I don't know."

That was also true. At that hour, it was still true.

I walked her to the door with the pretext of opening it and the motive of closing it. I promised to find her bibliography on builders' seals. I watched her car disappear toward Avenida Cuauhtémoc and stood a while on the threshold, smelling the morning. The city smelled of what it always smells of in February: dust waiting for rain the way one waits for back pay.

Then I closed the workshop at midday for the first time in eleven years, lowered the shutter, and went to the back room.

—

The back room holds a worktable for clients who do not exist and a safe that does. The safe is German, from 1911, bought new. I have never changed the combination, because the numbers mean nothing: the real safe is the habit of not opening it.

One hundred and eleven years of habit. I broke it in forty seconds.

Inside there is no money and there are no deeds. There is an object wrapped in linen, the size of a choir lectern, with the weight of stone and a temperature of its own: warm in winter, cool in the desert, always at the exact temperature of a hand that lets itself be held.

I set it on the table. I untied the linen. I won't pretend my hands weren't trembling; I stopped pretending anything in writing a long time ago.

The Register looks like a folio volume. That is its disguise for this era, as before it was a scroll, and before that a codex of boards, and at the beginning clay — baskets upon baskets of clay that traveled with me on donkeys that died of old age without my changing face. The leather of the binding comes from no animal that can be hunted. The cover says nothing. It has never needed to.

Inside are all of them.

Seven thousand two hundred and three names, one per page, in the order in which they swore. City gods and house gods, lords of storm and ladies of childbirth, the one who invented beer and the one who collected on oaths, entire pantheons with their hierarchies and their shames. Every page written in my hand, which changed alphabets over the centuries and never changed pulse. Most of the pages are closed: when a god dies, his leaf goes opaque and soft, like the skin of a slackened drum, and can no longer be written on. It can be caressed. I say so from experience.

Until that morning, the Register had seven living pages, and one of the seven was blank.

I did not go leaf by leaf. I didn't need to. The book knows how to fall open where it hurts, the way people do.

It opened at page eleven.

Page eleven belonged to Kulla, god of the brick. First to swear after the great ones, because the great ones spent three days arguing precedence and Kulla, who was small and practical, said he'd sign wherever, so long as he could get back to his site. Four thousand years surviving without a temple, without priests, without one single prayer addressed to him: sustained by the anonymous gesture of every bricklayer who beds a brick, levels it, gives it a tap with the handle of the trowel and looks at it for half a second with something he doesn't know is faith. Kulla, who taught me that the small gods last longest because they fit inside habits. Kulla, who smelled of wet earth and lime, and who the last time I saw him — Guanajuato, 1907, a quarry-stone job — told me, laughing, that he would lay the last brick of my tomb.

Page eleven was not opaque.

Page eleven was not there.

What remained was the stub, torn off three millimeters from the sewing, the fiber burst outward — outward: whoever tore it pulled from the spine, with knowledge — and with a cleanness that chilled me more than the body in the cement. I have restored mutilated books all my civil life. I know what a tear says. This one said: *without haste, without doubt, without the slightest intention of hiding it.*

I stayed looking at the gap for a length of time I did not measure — I, who measure everything.

And I want to leave in writing the first thing I did with that time, because it does not flatter me and for that very reason it must be on record: I looked for a way to make the guilt mine. I went back over my memory — which does not fail; it is part of the sentence that it not fail — hunting for a forgotten night, a lapse, an old woman's sleepwalking, any version of events in which I myself had torn out that page and didn't know it. I wished for it. I wished to be mad, which is the cheapest thing on the market of explanations, because a mad scribe was my problem, and the other thing — what that clean tear was saying at the top of its voice — was the world's.

My memory granted me nothing. It never grants me anything.

No one in the world knew where this book slept. No one but me, and I was in the workshop sewing a missal while someone, at some moment in the last one hundred and eleven years, opened my safe without opening it, took the Register without taking it, and tore a single page out of a book that cannot be destroyed.

It cannot be. That is the second law. I cannot destroy it, and I have tried with fire, with sea, and with a forge hammer, the week I closed the book and wanted to close everything.

I laid my palm on the stub of page eleven. The book was warm, as always. Like a hand that lets itself be held. Like a hand that let itself be held by someone else and didn't tell me.

"Who?" I asked aloud, in a language in which there is no one left to ask anything.

The Register does not answer. It has never answered. It registers.

I wrapped it again in its linen and did not return it to the safe. I left it on the table, in plain sight, because the hiding place had just lost its only merit. Then I went up for my coat, turned off the workshop lights, and paused a moment in the back room, amid the smell of rabbit-skin glue and old paper, to say the thing out loud so that it would be irremediable:

"Kulla is dead."

No one witnessed it but me. That is what I do for a living.

I went out to find a taxi to take me to see my friend's body, and the sky over the Doctores was the color of paper when it takes water: dun, bowed, about to give. In any other city I would have said it was going to rain. In this one, not yet. There were still people left to pray.
