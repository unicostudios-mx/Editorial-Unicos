# 12. The Doorman of Rome

Of the six dawns I slept at Mesones, the third was the one about Rome, and I tell it here because afterward there was no way to, and because the dead of this story keep claiming chapters for themselves the way grandparents claim the good rooms of a house.

It was four-thirty in the morning. The Lar was starting the next day's atole — champurrado on Tuesdays, "because chocolate lifts the week's spirits right where it sags" — and I watched him work from my bench, wrapped in a sheepskin blanket that smelled of rooftop sun. Outside, a garbage truck made its rounds. Inside, two thousand years of Roman history stirred a pot with a wooden spoon.

"Have I never told you about my family?" he said suddenly, without turning around.

"You've told me about every family in Rome except yours."

"Because porterías are told from the outside in." He tapped the spoon on the rim of the pot, twice, his liturgy. "But we're down to the last dawns, scribe. Don't tell me we're not — you know how to count too. So today it's mine."

—

His guild was born neither great nor small: it was born *exact*. One lar per house. Not per temple, not per city: per house. His own woke — that was his word: I woke, the way one wakes from a nap one doesn't remember starting — in a house on the Caelian Hill, in the years when Rome still fit on its hills, when the family living there first set a little altar by the door and left him, in a small clay dish, the first food of the new fire.

"The Prisci," he said. "Foot-soldier people with a cavalry name. The great-grandfather was the one who set out the dish. He didn't know my name; neither did I, for that matter: we lares have no names, we have an *address*. I was the lar of the house of the Prisci, and that was identity to spare."

He told me the mechanics of his kind, which I knew from the archive and never from the mouth: the altar by the fire, the little figures of bronze or wax, the daily offering — a bit of supper, flowers on feast days, the first cup of the new wine. Every house in Rome, hovel to palace, fed its lar. Add it up: in the good centuries, the guild of the lares was the best-paid and most invisible clergy in the world. No lar was ever great. All of them together were the greatest thing there was.

"We were the payroll of an empire, scribe. The ones above, the marble ones, fought over the temples and the parades. We had the other thing." He touched his chest with the spoon. "Supper. Every supper in Rome passed through us. Do you know what that is? Jupiter had the Capitol. I had the hour when the family sits down and looks at each other's faces. Trade me that if you can."

"And how does one die of that?" I asked, because it was the dawn for asking. "Theodosius never touched you. Household altars couldn't be shuttered with soldiers."

The Lar stirred the atole a while before answering, and when he answered the gossip was gone from his voice.

"The decree didn't kill us. The moving did." He set down the spoon. "A lar belongs to his house, scribe. Not to the stone: to the *inside*. And the inside of houses ran out little by little, with no edict at all: the family that scatters, the sons who leave for another province, the house that is sold, the one that collapses, the one that becomes a warehouse. Every move killed a lar or left him widowed. The great ones were killed by the hunger for faith. We were killed by the end of the sobremesa. It is a slower death with less chronicle, but it buries just the same."

The Prisci lasted him thirty generations. He told me the best ones with names, with trades, with shames — the one who gambled, the girl who ran off with a charioteer and came back three years later "and good for her, because the charioteer was a brute" — and at some point I realized I was hearing what no historian has ever had: the biography of an ordinary Roman family, complete, from founding to fade-out, told by the only witness who had supper with all of them.

"The last was Prisca. Like the first, imagine that. She ended up alone out of everyone, old, in the half-fallen house, and she kept setting out my little dish to the very end, even though by then setting out the dish was a misdemeanor and she was a daily-Mass Christian." The Lar smiled with his walnut wrinkles. "She set it out 'from habit,' she'd say out loud, in case her God was listening. And her God, as far as I know, never charged her for it. In that house I learned not to take offense at the blank page of your book, scribe: that Lord will have His mysteries, but He lets old women keep their habits, and in my guild that is the closest thing there is to good neighborliness."

"And when Prisca died?"

"I carried her supper to the door of the wake and there was no longer anyone to serve it to." He said it without drama, professionally. "That's when I was due to go out. Almost all of my kind went that way. But it didn't put me out, because I was already in your book. I had signed four centuries earlier, in the days of generation twenty-two, when I watched the first of the guild go out from a moving and a fear got into me that I confessed to no one: I went looking for you, I stood in line — there was a line, scribe; there were many of us small ones with fear, though you took notes so gravely nobody told you — and I signed. And your sewing did for me what no family could promise: it made me *constant*." He pointed the spoon at me. "That is why my oath is one word. You people swear long things when you are not sure. I was sure."

*Custodio.* A widowed household god wrote it in my book, in the cramped hand of a man used to taking down messages, and it is one of the ten signatures out of my seven thousand that I reread when the trade weighs on me.

—

He crossed the sea in 1621, in the luggage of a royal notary coming to New Spain with his whole clan. "I stowed away, why lie to you. The notary had a little altar of saints and I settled in behind them — the classic spot for my guild: behind the saints there's plenty of room and nobody dusts." And from family to family, zaguán to zaguán, he found what he calls, with no irony whatsoever, the finest theological invention of this continent:

"The vecindad, scribe. Listen well, because your book doesn't have this: the vecindad is the house with many families inside. Do you understand what that means to a lar? My entire species died by the arithmetic of one house, one family — and this country built houses of *twenty* families with a single zaguán." He threw his arms wide, the spoon dripping champurrado, the happiest god I have seen in three centuries. "I was widowed once in two thousand years. Here, when a family leaves me, I have nineteen left. Rome was never that smart."

I laughed. Let it stand in this notebook: at five in the morning on a Tuesday, in a portería on Mesones, the scribe of the gods laughed with the blanket up to her chin, and the atole smelled of cinnamon, and for a while the book I carry weighed nothing.

And then I asked the question I shouldn't have, because the small hours give courage for the cowardly questions, and this notebook already knows I collect them:

"Don't you hate me?" I said. "I closed the book that made you constant. You've spent a hundred years going out through a door I shut, and you take me in with atole and tuck me under your best blanket. The Prisci lasted you thirty generations. I failed you in one."

The last lar of Rome put out the flame, covered the pot, and sat down across from me in his doorman's chair, the one that faces the entrance, the chair in which days later he would die without giving up his shift.

"Scribe," he said: "I have kept doors for two thousand years, and at the doors you learn one large thing only. People close out of weariness and open out of love, and nobody — hear me — *nobody* does both on time. Prisca set out my dish to the end and never once said come in. You closed the book on us, and here you are, asleep on my cot six nights running with your sheepskin blanket, looking after an old man who can no longer be looked after." He shrugged. "Hate you? I am a doorman. I don't judge the ones who arrive late. I just open the door."

He stayed on watch, as every dawn. I fell asleep to the sound of the garbage truck and its bell, and that time, I swear it upon the book, it did ring like a temple.
