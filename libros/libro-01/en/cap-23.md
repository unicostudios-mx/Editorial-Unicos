# 23. The Night of the Gods

From the Register. México-Tenochtitlan, 1521. And the hill.

I crossed the sea in 1520, on a ship that smelled of pitch and greed, because news had reached Seville of a new world, and the news carried, among the gold and the islands, the one word that puts me on the road: gods.

I must begin with a guild confession: my book was, until that year, an Old World book. Seven thousand pages, and not one from this side of the water. Not from disdain: from distance. Those above do not cross oceans their believers do not cross, and I go where they go. So I came to these shores as I had come to India: believing I came to inscribe, and I was the one inscribed.

What I found was not a pantheon. It was an *administration of the cosmos* in full operation, finer than anything I had seen since Egypt: gods with a double calendar, with shifts, with a budget of blood and flower carefully accounted, sustained by a people who understood — better than any other I had ever dealt with — the central truth of my trade: that those above are fed, and that feeding costs. The sages of these lands knew with exactness what Greece suspected and Rome forgot: that the sun does not rise for free.

And I arrived in time to watch it fall.

I will not recount the war here; your books carry it, from both sides, and where both sides agree the truth usually sits: the months of the siege, the hunger, the thirst of the lake city with its aqueduct cut, the smallpox that killed more than the swords — and that killed upward just as evenly, because a god whose people die at that speed is a god bleeding out through ten thousand wounds not his own. Cuauhtémoc defended what could no longer be defended and was taken prisoner on an August 13th; that stands on record in both tongues. What stands on record in neither, I write, because this is what I have lasted for:

That night, over the broken city, those above of this world held their last assembly. I was not invited — I was a foreigner to both sides, the scribe of the gods who came on the ships — but my book is my safe-conduct, and they let me watch from the edge, as the devas did at Nalanda. I will not recount what I saw in detail, out of respect for a mourning that was not mine. I will say what matters to this chronicle: they did not surrender. They deliberated. With their city burnt and their blood-accounting broken, they deliberated like the administration they were, and they took the most lucid decision I have ever seen an entire sky take:

To disperse and transform. To stop being buildings.

They had not read Serapis. There was no need. The same hunger teaches the same school. The great ones of this world portioned themselves out that night into the small things that survive: into the hills nobody can burn, into the caves of the water, into the seeds, into the customs that fit inside other customs the way a letter fits inside a second envelope. The friars who came after spent centuries persecuting "hidden idolatries" without understanding that they were not persecuting a resistance: they were persecuting a *move*, already completed, to a domicile that has no address.

From the months of the siege I keep one record that is in no chronicle of either side, and I set it here because this book collects exactly these: one moonless night, out toward Iztapalapa, I saw a canoe cross the lake loaded with children — they were being taken out of the blockade, as so few could be taken. The brigantine patrols were sweeping the water; the canoe was headed straight for one. And the lake, along a stretch of a hundred fathoms, filled with fog. A low fog, thick, absurd for the season, that lasted exactly as long as the canoe took to pass and then came apart the way breath comes off glass. The patrol cursed it the way one curses weather. I stayed watching the water, because I do know what a fog out of season costs on a dying lake: I watched it being paid for. I felt someone of the water — I will not say who; that name has owners — go dark to himself for a long while, emptied, the way a house goes when it pawns its last good piece of furniture. One canoe of children, against the fall of a world. The gods of this land could no longer save their city. They could, that night, with everything one of them had left, buy a hundred fathoms of fog. In my archive, ever since, those two sentences are worth the same — I said it once of another water and another gangplank, and I hold to it: the miracles of defeat are not measured by size. They are measured by price.

Two of them, no more, also wished to sign my book.

The first was the old fire. Huehuetéotl, the wrinkled one, lord of the center of the hearth, who was ancient already when the Mexica came to the valley and had watched the peoples pass the way firewood passes. He came to my camp on the shore, the size of any grandfather, sat down in my coals — inside them, with the naturalness of a man taking his own chair — and told me: "I have moved house six times already, scribe. I know how it's done. But I am too old now to move without papers. Write me down, so that even if I lose myself, it will not be lost that I was." He signed with a small ember, burning his name into the page, which is the only fire signature my book has.

The second arrived the next day, when the old fire told him. He came with the low clouds of August, a short, solid señor who smelled of wet earth, and he was not fleeing: he was *calculating*, as his kind calculate, by long cycles. "Water goes in after the flame, so as not to put it out," he said, yielding the turn with that rainy-season courtesy I would come to know in him across five centuries. And before signing he asked me the only technical question a god has ever asked me about the contract: "Does your book hold up in water?" I told him it had survived three shipwrecks. He nodded, satisfied, like a man contracting for a raincoat, and signed.

I stayed in this land a long century. I did not decide it: I kept deciding it, which is how long things are decided. Here was happening the thing my guild cares about: the greatest experiment in divine survival in history, the moving of entire heavens in real time. And here, besides, was the hill.

The census of the move took me decades, and it was the finest fieldwork I have done in four millennia, because for the first time my trade consisted not of finding gods but of finding *hiding places*. I learned to read them. I learned that when a town throws a grand fiesta for a newly arrived saint, you must look at which hill the chapel faces and where the best part of the food goes at night. I learned that there are dances in which the one who dances with the deer's head is not representing anything: he is *lending the body*, one afternoon a year, with everyone's permission and no one's knowledge. I learned that a lord of the mountain can live for generations inside a mayordomía, passed from office to office the way a child is passed from arm to arm, fed by a devotion that in the parish ledgers has another addressee. The finest friars smelled it always — I have already cited the distrust of Sahagún, who was the best nose of his century — and a century later a licenciado of the hot lands gathered into a treatise the spells he pried out of people by force of patience and lashes, scandalized that in them the tobacco called itself by flowered names and the water answered to a lady's titles. His book is in your libraries. I have read it with the care with which one reads the report of an honorable enemy: the licenciado believed he was taking a census of superstitions. He was drawing up, without knowing it, the directory of the move: the list of code names under which those above of this land went on collecting, little by little, at the window next door.

And I must also set down the price, because the move tells easily and it was not easy: the clandestinity of those above was paid for by those below. In 1539 I stood in the great plaza of this city on the morning they burned a lord of Texcoco, grandson of the poet king, raised by the friars themselves, lettered in both tongues. The case file exists; it is in your archives; you can read what he was accused of: keeping in his house what his grandfather left him, and saying aloud that every people had its own cloak and its own way. I watched him burn with the composure of his caste, and I watched the full plaza, and I watched the Indians of the plaza watch it all with a silence the friars took for a lesson learned — and that I, who have spent four millennia reading silences, read as what it was: the last lesson of the move, learned in another's flesh. *Not in the house. Not in the voice. Deeper in.* After him, the tribunal never again tried Indians for their faith — the scandal lasted them forever; that too is on record — and it is the only tribunal I have watched back away ashamed in four thousand years. Don Carlos is not registered in my book, because my book does not register mortals, and what that outstanding account cost me I have said in another chapter. I register him here, where I am permitted: a man died for the crime of inheriting. Let it be on record who paid for the hiding place.

To very few did I offer inscription in those decades, and nearly all of them answered me the same, each in their own way: no. The clearest was a lord of the small clouds, a younger cousin of the one who later signed, who received me in his cave of offerings with corn of three colors and said, pointing at the hill with his chin: "Your book is a book of names, scribe. We have already agreed to leave the name at the door. He who moves house with his name and all did not move: he only changed address so they could find him." Four millennia of contracts, and the best-stated theory of clandestinity I know was said to me by a lord of the rains with his mouth full of esquites. That is why my census of this land has so many pages of signs and so few of names, and why it is incomplete with pride: it is the only census I have ever taken where the gap is not my defeat. It is their victory.

—

Of the hill I must write slowly, because this is the page where lying comes easiest, and this book does not let me, and even so there are fine ways of lying that consist of choosing what to tell. I am going to tell all I know, which is little, and to point at the gap, which is large.

North of the city, by the lake, there was a low hill called Tepeyac, and on it, since long before the Mexica, a shrine of the mother. Tonantzin, said those who climbed: *our little mother*. It was not a proper name but a title, and several ladies of above bore it, as happens with mothers, who are many and are one. I climbed that hill in 1523, in my first census of the move, and found the shrine already thrown down by the friars and the hill bare, and in the air of the hill... I note exactly what I noted that afternoon: *presence without domicile. Like a house freshly swept, expecting a visit.*

What happened afterward is in your documents and in your disputes, and I will not be the one to settle them. It is on record that by mid-century, on that same hill, there stood a new hermitage and an enormous devotion to the Virgin Mary under an advocation the whole world now knows. It is on record that the naturales climbed by the tens of thousands, from leagues away, as they had always climbed, and that they called the Lady, in their tongue, *Tonantzin*. And it is on record — in the pen of the greatest of the scholar friars, Sahagún, who loved this people and distrusted this hill — the complaint: that the thing smelled of an old cult under a new name, and that no one could be certain to whom so many people were praying.

I went. Of course I went. I went for decades, with my book, among the pilgrims.

And here is the gap, marked out with both my hands:

I did not find on the Tepeyac any lady of my census. Not one of the mothers of this world whom I had seen and counted in the move. If one of them hid there, she hid better than any of the seven thousand I have known, and I have spent four millennia learning how those above hide.

Nor did I find emptiness. The hill was not like the white city of the extinguished Aten, nor like the temples of the great famine. It was *inhabited*. There was there an attention — I will not name it further, because nouns affirm, and here I affirm nothing — something that received those tens of thousands and did not eat them. I underline this, because it is the only technical datum I can offer and I have not seen it at any other sanctuary in the world in four thousand years: all devotion feeds someone; that is how the whole sky works; it is the first law of my trade. And I, on the Tepeyac, with the book in my hands, could never measure *where* the food was going. Faith rose as it rises everywhere, and it fattened no one in my census, and it was not lost. It went somewhere my accounting does not reach.

I offered inscription, as was my duty. One single time, one December morning, with the hill full and the candles lit. And I received what I received at Nicaea, and in the hall of the empty Temple: the certainty of being read back, whole, to the bottom, and declined without discourtesy. The same signature without signature. The same kind weight.

Draw what conclusion you please; conclusions are not in short supply in this matter: the friars', the scholars', those of the millions who climb every December and need my accounting for nothing at all. My book admits only what is on record, and what is on record is this: on the hill to the north there is a page of this land that resembles my blank page. I do not say they are the same. I do not say they are not. I say that both let themselves be loved and do not let themselves be written, and that a scribe knows when a silence is a closed door and when it is a door that is looking at you.

The Tepeyac's looks.

I came down that December morning with my book unused, among thousands coming down with shining eyes, and an already old woman, barefoot, coming down beside me, saw my working face and said to me, with all the compassion in the world, in Nahuatl:

"She didn't grant you, madrecita?"

"I didn't come to ask," I answered. "I came to write down."

She nodded, very serious, like someone familiar with the problem, and said the sentence with which I will close this chapter, because in five centuries I have found none better, and because it was said by a barefoot old woman and not by a theologian, which is how this land says its best things:

"Ay, hija. There are things that are not written down. That's why we come to see them."
