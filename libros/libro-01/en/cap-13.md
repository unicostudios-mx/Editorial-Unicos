# 13. The End of the Temples

From the Register. Alexandria, year 391.

Of all the gods I have inscribed, Serapis was the only one who knew what he was, and that lucidity cost him and served him in equal parts.

He was manufactured. I do not say it as an insult: I say it as a matter of record. When the first Ptolemy inherited Egypt from Alexander's wars, he found himself with an administrative problem the size of two civilizations: Greeks and Egyptians in the same city, each with their own gods, and no appetite for sharing altars. And the king did what good kings do with practical matters: he convened a commission. An Egyptian priest, a Greek theologian of the Eumolpid family, experts, a budget. Out of it came Serapis: Greek beard, Zeus's manners, Osiris's trade, a grain basket on his head so the people would understand the business was abundance. A god by design, made to unify the market.

I went to inscribe him with more curiosity than respect, I confess it here where I cannot lie. I came out two hours later with the respect multiplied and the curiosity intact, which is the best ratio I know.

Because Serapis *knew*. He signed my book and told me, with a frankness no earthborn god would have permitted himself:

"We are both artisanry, scribe. You were made by a commission of gods; I, by a commission of men. The difference is that I harbor no illusions about my origin, and that, you will see, is a theological advantage."

And it was. While the old gods administered their decline with the blind dignity of aristocracy, Serapis administered his success like the businessman he was: branch temples, franchises of the cult, healing miracles with office hours. His headquarters, the Serapeum of Alexandria, became one of the largest things faith has ever built: an artificial acropolis atop a hundred steps, with a library of its own — the daughter of the great one — and the god inside, colossal, of precious woods and metals, with that Alexandrian craftsmen's technique that made the eyes seem to follow you. They followed. He lived there. A god by design understands that the image is not his portrait: it is his domicile.

When the general hunger began — the one after Nicaea, the slow one, the administered one — Serapis was among those who read it best. He told me in his last good decade, strolling through his library:

"This is not a war, Enheduanna, and my kind are wrong to prepare for a war. Wars are won by the strongest. This is a change of administration. Changes of administration are won by whoever signs the inventory." He stopped in front of his own scrolls. "They will come for the building. Not for me: for the building. What does not enter the inventory does not exist for the new administrator, and we are, to them, buildings. You should warn the little ones: let them learn, starting now, not to be buildings."

*Let them learn not to be buildings.* I wrote it down that night. Of everything those above have ever said to me in four millennia, it is the most exact survival instruction, and it was given by a god manufactured by commission.

—

The decrees arrived the way decrees arrive: first as rumor, then as copy, then as soldiers. The emperor Theodosius had been closing the tap year by year — sacrifices forbidden, temple visits forbidden, forbidden to look at the statues *the way one looks at a god*, which is the finest and most ferocious prohibition any bureaucrat ever drafted. In Alexandria the matter heated up the way everything heats up in Alexandria: with a riot. There were dead on both sides, there were pagans barricaded in the Serapeum, there was a bishop, Theophilus, who understood power the way Serapis understood commerce, and there was, at the end, the imperial authorization everyone knew would come.

I was on the great stairway that day, in the crowd, the book against my chest. A hundred steps of people: soldiers and the bishop's monks above, the whole city below, and between them the taut air of endings that no one can administer anymore.

And what happened is what the Christian chronicles themselves record with pride, and I confirm it word for word because I saw it: the soldiers hesitated. Before the colossus, axe in hand, the men of the most powerful empire in the world hesitated, because all Alexandria had known since childhood that if a hand touched Serapis, the earth would split open and the sky would fall in pieces. Then one soldier — the chronicles do not give his name; neither do I: he was a man with fear, which is the commonest name in the world — climbed the scaffold, took his swing, and brought the axe down on the god's cheek.

The blow sounded like what it was: old wood. The cheek came away, spun in the air, fell bouncing down the hundred steps — a piece of a god's face bouncing down a stairway; that sound has not left me in sixteen centuries — and out of the opened wood came, before ten thousand witnesses, a family of rats that had lived for years inside the head of Serapis.

The earth did not split open. The sky did not fall.

Something worse fell, and I felt it fall across the whole stairway, in the breathing of ten thousand people at once: the *just in case* fell. That last coin of faith — the unbeliever's, who still does not touch the statue; the convert's, who still nods from afar to the old god. The just-in-case fed half of divine Alexandria, and that axe cut it in one stroke. I have watched gods die of hunger and gods die of oblivion; that afternoon I watched an entire city stop being afraid in the same minute, and I do not know how that is registered, so I register it thus: the silence after the blow lasted three breaths and was worth a century of drought.

They dismantled the colossus over the rest of the day, with saws, with ropes, with method. They distributed the pieces through the neighborhoods to burn them in public, one per oven, which is the exact liturgy of the inventory: not to destroy the building, but to demonstrate that it was a building.

I found Serapis that night at the harbor, sitting on a bollard, the size of a tired stevedore. He was watching one of his pieces burn in the distance.

"Don't look at me with mourning, scribe, I know it by heart," he said. "They were right about everything, you know. It was wood. It was always wood. I sat on the commission, I knew the carpenter — an excellent man, fond of Chian wine." He shrugged. "Their error is not one of fact, it is one of inventory: they believe that by proving the wood they proved me. I did not live *in* the wood; I lived in the just-in-case. And that, yes — that they burned today. That cannot be restored."

"How long do you have?" I asked him, because he could be asked that way, between professionals.

"Little, and poor in quality." He smiled with his whole cheek — he could. "I have the jokes left. Do you know one is already going around the markets? That there were rats inside my head, and that's why the oracles came out mangy. It's good. It was invented by the same people who yesterday brought me their sick children." He watched the far-off fire. "The joke is a god's last rent, Enheduanna. It pays in copper, but it pays. When they no longer even mock — then, yes, start closing my page."

The copper lasted him twenty years. His page closed one winter afternoon, on its own, under my hand, while I copied Homer on commission, and in the market next door nobody remembered the joke anymore.

—

The years after the Serapeum I spent taking the census of the closing, and I did it with a travel guide under my arm, and I want to leave on record the book and its author, because my guild pays its debts: two centuries before the decrees, a Greek traveler named Pausanias had walked his own country writing everything down — every temple, every image, who carved it, what festival they held for it, what the village sacristan used to tell. Your scholars use him to excavate. I used him to bury. I went along the roads of Greece with his book, collating his present against mine: where he had noted processions I found thistles; where he had heard the oracle, the wind made the sound wind makes in hollow things. Even in his own day, let it be said, the traveler had seen the drought coming — he notes roofless sanctuaries, silenced oracles, villages where only the god's name remained, the way a stain remains on the wall when the picture is taken down — and even so he wrote it all down, with that piety of inventories made in time. At every ruin I opened his page and mine, the description and the death record, the living house and the swept one, and by the third or fourth time I understood what book his really was, and why my hands trembled to carry it: the traveler of the twilight had drawn up, without knowing it, the register of the domiciles, as I kept the register of the tenants. Between the two books fit everything that was lost. No pair of books should be able to say that, and I say it — I, who am one of the two.

The hardest station of that census was Eleusis, and I register it apart, because at Eleusis I learned the exception to the law I did not yet know I was going to write. For one thousand eight hundred years, in that town by the sea, the mysteries were celebrated: the oldest and most honored initiation of the Greek world, that of the Mother and the Maiden, of the grain that goes down and returns. Emperors stood in line to be initiated. And its content — what was seen in the night of the telesterion, what sent people home changed — was never written. Not one line in eighteen centuries: the best-kept secret of the species, sustained by oath, generation upon generation, and I attest that it held, because they did not tell even me, and I offered inscription twice. When the Goths of the north came down upon Greece, five years after the Serapeum, with men in black walking among their columns — a pagan sage of the era left that in writing; look it up — the sanctuary burned, and was not rebuilt, and the last legitimate hierophant had prophesied, his own circles say, that he would see with his eyes the end of the mysteries. He saw it.

And here is the exception, which took me centuries to phrase: the told gods are sustained by the telling — that law I would write a thousand years later in Florence. But what was never told has no second life. Of Zeus the poems remain; of Aphrodite, the marbles; even of Pan there remained the word panic, collecting rent on every fright. Of the night of the telesterion there is nothing left to warm. The secret died whole, with the last one who swore it, and its hollow in the world is not like the other hollows, which have the shape of what is missing. This one has no shape. Your scholars have spent centuries trying to reconstruct what was seen on that night, and they fail with elegance, and I read them failing, knowing what I know: that absolute silence is the only complete death there is, and that Greece, which told the world everything, kept exactly one thing to itself, and that is the only thing it truly lost. Since Eleusis I say it thus in my guild: telling is life insurance. Whoever wants no second life, let them not tell. But let them decide it knowingly.

From that census I also kept a thorn I want to set down because it explains something about me that this notebook needs. In Athens the great one was gone: the Parthenos of gold and ivory, Phidias's, which for eight centuries was the most famous body a god ever inhabited, had been carried off by the new administrators to their new capital, as a collector's piece, which is the refined fate of the inventoried. I tracked her professionally for years: it is on record that she stood in Constantinople, displayed among other captive masterworks, admired by people who no longer owed her anything. And then — search your sources; I searched mine and yours: nothing. No fire with a date, no sack with a culprit, no destruction with a decree. The archives simply stop mentioning her. At some point in the centuries, the body of the goddess who presided over the Parthenon leaves the last list and enters no other, and that administrative silence is the whole of her ending. The goddess, I clarify, was already in my closed pages; I speak of the body, the famous domicile, and I speak of it because there I learned which genus of death my guild cannot forgive or forgive itself: not the violent death, nor the early one, nor the unjust one. Death *without mention*. Falling off the lists. Not that the world destroy you: that it stop writing you. Everything I did afterward — every record, every graft, every line of this notebook — can be read as one scribe's war against that genus. And if anyone wants to know why my own final entry mattered so much to me, let them come back to this paragraph: I saw what happens to the most beautiful thing in the world when no one writes its ending.

That was the year 391 and its decade: not a battle — an inventory. Of the thousands in my book, the ones who survived that century were exactly the ones who understood in time the manufactured god's instruction: the ones who knew how to stop being buildings. The small ones, the household ones, the ones who could fit inside a habit: in lighting the stove of the sad, in blessing the threshold, in the bricklayer's little tap on the seated brick. The fire and the brick beat the colossi, and no theologian above or below has it in their manuals yet.

And there remained, from that afternoon on the stairway, my working note, which I reread every time this century boasts to me of some demolition:

*The axe did not kill the god. What killed him was the absence of trembling.*
