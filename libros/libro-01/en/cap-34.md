# 34. The Vigil

The call came on a Sunday at seven in the morning, from the landline of the house of studies. It was the coordinator, a young woman who apologized three times for the hour, and her message fit in one sentence she did not know was a sentence out of the suttas:

"It's just that don Sergio is acting strange, señora Edna. You're his friend, aren't you? Yesterday he was watering the garden and he stopped halfway, hose in hand, and told us: 'I no longer find myself here.' Just like that. 'I no longer find myself.' And today he wouldn't sit on his little bench."

The fifth sign. The deva no longer delights in his seat.

"I'm on my way," I said. "And don't worry: he isn't ill. He is finishing a very long piece of work."

I counted seven days, as he taught me. It was six. Even in that he was punctual to a fault, my king: all his life he arrived early to everything, and death was not going to be the exception that disordered his service record.

—

I installed myself in the little room beside the kitchen that same morning, with what was needed: the Register, my good ink, two pens in case one failed — it was not going to fail; I checked them nine times — and the three-legged bench where I sat for six days, in the exact spot where I sat the afternoon of the tea, because he asked for it so: "where you are sitting now."

The house was told that don Sergio was delicate and that I was his person. The young people of the center took it with a naturalness that moved me: they organized shifts to bring us food, lowered their voices in the corridor, and the teacher who directed the place — a serious man who had spent years giving don Sergio that deep bow he could not explain to himself — came up the first day, stood in the doorway, looked at the dying gardener and the señora with the enormous book, and instead of asking anything, said: "We'll be reciting downstairs. In case it helps." It helped. All that week there rose up the stairwell, steady as a tide, the murmur of the doctrine that best described the world: *all composite things decay; work on with diligence.* Śakra listened with his eyes closed and sometimes moved his lips a little, correcting the Pali pronunciation, out of the sheer old teacher he was.

I wrote his death while it happened. Line by line, in the present, the first new entry of my book in one hundred and eleven years. I will not copy it here in full: it is his, it stands on his page, and the records of kings are read in their archive, not in the memoirs of their scribes. I will tell, with his eternal permission, what did not fit in the record. That is what memoirs are for.

—

The first day he talked about his palaces. Not with nostalgia: with inventory. The Vejayanta of a thousand stories, the gardens where the coral tree flowered once per divine century and the perfume lasted what an entire human generation lasts down here. "It was never that I missed most," he said. "It was the elephant. Erāvaṇa. When we came down together, the whole sky moved aside. A king does not know what he is until he is carried by someone who could choose not to carry him."

The second day he interrogated me. Six hours, with a king's method: my father, Ur, the salt flats, Amalia. At the end he ruled, with the only whole smile of the week: "Four thousand years and two loves. You are an austere administration, scribe. I had a thousand consorts and the good conversations I had with an ascetic under a tree. I don't know which of us made the worse bargain."

The third day he told me about the Awakened One. I keep it apart, because it was the only time that week his voice trembled. He told me the visit his own texts preserve: how he came down, being king of the Thirty-Three, with a question that embarrassed him — "envy and avarice, scribe; not even the thunderbolt kills those; I know it firsthand" — and how the man under the tree answered him step by step, without ceremony, the way one explains a knot to a colleague, until the knot gave. "That day I entered the stream," he said. "Do you know what that means? That this" — he touched his chest, where the light was already the thickness of a sheet of paper — "is not my last line in the ledger. I have lives left, few, and none of them will be a king's, thank all the heavens. The Awakened One took away my fear with arithmetic. No one had ever given me a gift like that: an *account*."

The fourth day he slept almost entirely, and I wrote in the present the sleep of a deva, which is one of the things there are no human words for, so the page says, in my best hand: *He sleeps. The room still smells, faintly, of flower without flower. Every so often he smiles. I don't know at whom.*

The fifth day he asked to be seated by the window, facing the garden, and spent the afternoon taking leave of the hummingbirds by name. They had names. All of them. He had named them in secret over the years, in Pali, "because the gardener writes the labels, scribe — it is the privilege of the post."

And on the night of the fifth day, when the reciting downstairs had gone quiet and the house slept, he asked me the thing I was not expecting.

"You are writing a book of your own," he said, eyes closed. "Not the Register. The other one. The blue-covered one you carry in your bag and touch when you think nobody sees. We dying see everything, scribe; it is our last benefit." He opened one eye, the exact measure of theater. "Read me something from it. The brick one's part, if you have it. I liked the brick one. He leveled true."

So on the night of the fifth day, in the little room beside the kitchen, I read a dying king the first chapter of this book — the one about the god in the cement, the torn-out stub, the city smelling of dust that waits. My voice trembled at the start, and I did not fix it, because he himself had taught me, two chapters back in his account and six days back in mine, that the trembling is also part of the record.

When I finished he stayed so still I thought him asleep. He was not asleep. He was ruling, which in kings is a different posture.

"It is well written," he said at last, with the exact solemnity of his best edicts. "It has two beautiful sentences too many and it fails to say you were afraid, but the doctor will correct that for you — that's what you trained her for." A long pause, of the kind that already cost him. "Read it to everyone, Enheduanna. When you and I finish here. To the living and to those who come. We die better since you write things down; perhaps they will live better for the same reason." And already almost from inside sleep, with the last irony he had left: "And make me handsome in my chapter. I had thirty thousand elephants. Let it be on record."

It is on record, lord. And the garden, and the gathered cups, and the labels of the privilege of the post. All of it is on record. That is what I was sentenced for; this is what I stayed for.

And on the sixth day, at nightfall, when the murmur downstairs changed suttas without anyone telling them — mortals know; the body of the world knows — the king of the Thirty-Three opened his eyes, searched for me, found my hand already resting on his page, the pen loaded, the line begun, and said:

"Are you writing?"

"I am writing."

"While?"

"While."

"Read me the last line."

I read it to him: *Evening falls. The king watches the garden. The house recites. It is on record.*

"On record," he repeated, savoring the word he himself chose for his oath eight hundred years ago, in a burnt orchard, his library blazing in front of him. And then, with the last light of a lamp that burned twenty thousand years on its own oil, the lord of the thunderbolt, the one who questioned the Awakened One and received an answer, the gardener of colonia Roma, gave me the final commission, in a voice so low that only the pen and I heard it: "Write: *he did not die alone*. In those words. It is all I wanted from the universe on my last stretch, and it was given me by a scribe of another guild. Let it be on record for the devas who read your book someday that it can be done. That looking away is not law. That it is only fear with good manners."

"*He did not die alone,*" I wrote, and said it aloud as I wrote it, so that the entry and the room would be the same place.

He nodded, satisfied, closed his eyes, and left the way fragrance leaves: without a border, without an instant that can be pointed to, so that the pen ran on one line more out of pure fidelity, and the line reads, and so it stays: *He is leaving. He left. The garden goes on. Erāvaṇa, if you are out there: come down for him, let the sky move aside.*

The page closed itself under my hand, opaque and soft, warm one moment longer than the others, as if merit took a little extra time to cool off the paper. Downstairs, the recitation ended at the usual hour, not knowing it had been a state funeral, the last of its kind. And in the darkened garden, against the pirul, a man was standing.

I did not hear him arrive. He is not heard. He had been there, I understand now, all week, at the exact edge where he stood at Nalanda, where he has stood at every ending since before my book: not intervening, not hurrying, keeping the other shift of the workshop. When I came out into the garden with the book against my chest, Māra bowed his head toward the window of the little room — a deep bow, the one I never saw him give anything in eight hundred years — and then toward me. He did not say "I told you so." Grant him that: he never says I told you so. He lets you say it inside yourself, which is more efficient.

Because halfway down the line — he knew it; hence his farewell at the workshop — I had understood: this was his offer. To write the deaths with my own hand, to accompany them to their water, to finish the book line by line. The acts were exactly the ones he had proposed to me in exchange for a sentence. I understood it with the pen wet, writing *he did not die alone*, and understood in the same motion why he will never win this argument even though he is not wrong about the facts, and I told him so from the garden door, in a low voice, because wakes are no place for debate but they are a place for records:

"You were right, Māra. They are the same acts." The pirul moved with the air; the still man did not. "But you offered them as payment for a confession, and I did them as the answer to a commission. Same verb, different grammar. And in my guild, grammar is everything: it changes who the subject is. In your version, the subject was you. In this week's version, it was he. *It is on record. He asks nothing more.* He never asked more. And it is on record for him."

Māra stayed a moment longer, watching the window where the coordinator, inside, was arranging don Sergio's hands with a granddaughter's care. And for the first and only time in this story, the lord of impermanence answered me without an argument, with four words I have still not finished weighing, and which I register here because they weigh:

"That is why you last."

He left through the garden without stepping on the shadows, or stepping on all of them — with him one never knows. And I went back inside, to keep vigil over my king with the mortals who loved him without knowing who he was, which is the only way anyone ever loved him, and the best.

On his little bench in the garden, the house put up a small brass plaque the next day that reads "Don Sergio, gardener of this house," with this century's dates, the false ones, his. Below it, the serious teacher had a line engraved in Pali, "because it came to me, I don't know," he told me, not knowing he was quoting a king's oath:

*It is on record.*
