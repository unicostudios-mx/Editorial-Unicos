# 11. The Blank Page

From the Register. Nicaea, one thousand seven hundred years ago. And before.

I have inscribed seven thousand two hundred and three names. I have watched lords of the storm swear with the lightning still in their hands, and little latrine gods sign weeping with gratitude because no one, ever, had asked them for their full name. My book does not discriminate: it registers whatever lets itself be registered.

There is one page that has no name. It sits between page one hundred sixty and page one hundred sixty-one, sewn by my hands, and it has been blank for seventeen centuries. It is the only page of the Register that cannot be written on. It is also the only one that cannot be torn out, and on that second property depends, as will be seen, the entire ending of this story. But I am getting ahead. First I must tell how an empty page earned its place, and for that I have to confess the longest failure of my career.

—

I first learned of Him in Babylon, in the years of Judah's exile. I was working then as a copyist in the tablet house, and the deportees from Jerusalem moved along the canals with a way of carrying defeat that made me look twice: they carried no images. Every exile I have ever seen — and I have seen them all — carries their gods in bundles, hides them in the bread dough, disguises them as a comb or a mirror. These people carried scrolls. Their god fit in a text and therefore did not weigh, and therefore could not be confiscated, and I, a scribe after all, felt that guild respect one does not choose.

I asked, with my thousand-year manners, where I might pay my respects to their lord. An old man of theirs looked at me a long time and gave me the first of the sentences I would collect over the following centuries:

"He is not anywhere, woman. That is why they could not burn Him."

I came to understand it slowly — understanding was my job: a God without a statue, who forbade the statue. A God whose name was not pronounced, written in letters that were a warning more than a sound. A God who shared no altar, negotiated no precedence, signed no treaties with His neighbors above — and believe me, my book is full of treaties His neighbors wanted to offer Him. Everything I knew about gods was made of form, place, and name. Here was Someone who had renounced all three, or had never needed them, and who instead of eating testimony like everyone seemed to... I don't know the verb, and I have spent twenty-five centuries looking for it. *Self-sustaining* is the least inexact thing I have.

Centuries later, when the Roman general Pompey took Jerusalem, he did what generals do: he entered where he should not. He drew aside the veil of the most sacred precinct of the Temple, the place where any people in the world kept their fattest god, and what he found, your own historians left in writing: nothing. An empty room. *The seat vacant and the mysteries vain,* wrote Tacitus, with that Roman disdain that was their way of crossing themselves. All Rome laughed at the people who worshipped an empty room.

I did not laugh. By then I had spent two thousand years inscribing gods, and I could read an empty room better than any general: it was not that no one was there. It was that Someone was not *on display*. All my science was the science of the witness, and I stood before the first Lord who accepted no witnesses. Who had them — millions, faithful as few are — but did not *need* them, and that difference, which sounds like a nuance, is the size of an abyss. I feed everyone who signs my book: my page is the last believer of every god. What could my page offer to One who does not eat of that?

I tried anyway. Four times in four centuries, with all the protocol humility my sentence allows me. I will not detail the attempts; the book lets me keep silent, and there are silences that are the only reverence available. I will state the result, which was the same all four times, and which was nothing like the Aten. With the Aten there was no interlocutor: it was like swearing before noon itself. Here there was something else. There was the certainty — physical, at the nape, in the soles of the feet — of being read back. Of my offer being heard, understood to the bottom, down to layers of my trade that I myself do not know, and declined. Without a voice, without a sign, without the least discourtesy. The way a king declines a coin: without touching it.

The fourth time, my book trembled. It is the only time in four thousand years. I have never told it and I write it now with both hands on the table: the Register, which has lain open through battles of the sky and funerals of entire pantheons without moving a fiber, trembled like an apprentice. Then I stopped trying. There are guilds, I learned, that greet each other from afar.

—

And yet I sewed the page. I will explain why, because it is the most disputed decision of mine in this book, and the few who knew of it disputed it, every one.

It was at Nicaea, in the year 325 of your era.

I was there. In the register of men that neither appears nor would have reason to: one more copyist among the dozens the bishops brought, an older woman who prepared ink and sharpened reeds on the benches at the back. Councils resemble the assemblies of gods more than either party could bear to know: the same fight over precedence, the same thin ones seated beside the fat ones, the same three-day exhaustion turned into formulas. What was being argued, at bottom, was a single thing: what exactly the Son was with respect to the Father, and all of Christendom was being wagered on one Greek word — on one letter's difference inside that word. I have watched empires founded and sunk; I have never seen so much weight on a letter.

It is not my place to judge what was decided there, and my book does not register it either: my book registers gods who swear, and the One the bishops spoke of had not sworn and was not going to. But one night of the council, with the lamps low and the scribes asleep over the drafts of the creed, I stayed alone in the chapter hall with my book in my lap, and for the first time in centuries I did not offer inscription.

I offered something else. I said, in my dead language, in a low voice, to the empty hall that no longer seemed empty to me:

"I do not ask You to sign. I understand that You do not sign. But I am the memory of those above, and Yours say You are above, and my book will outlast me. If someday You want something to be on record — whatever it is, whenever it is — there will be a place."

And I sewed the blank page that night, between one hundred sixty and one hundred sixty-one, with waxed thread and my best hands.

There was no sign. Do not expect signs in this story; I have gone seventeen centuries without one and I do not complain. There was, yes, a technical discovery, made the next morning by a sleepy scribe who wanted to test a new ink in a margin: the page does not take ink. The drop slides off whole, leaving no shadow, like water on wax. Stylus, blood, candle flame: the page admits no mark. And when I wanted — out of professional rigor, with my heart pounding — to test whether I could at least detach it and sew it in better, my fingers could not find *how*. There was no resistance. There was an absence of handhold. The blank page does not let itself be written, does not let itself be torn out, and does not explain.

I have thought everything that can be thought about it, and my trade obliges me to leave on record the three readings among which I have not known how to choose in one thousand seven hundred years:

That the page is empty the way the room of the Temple was empty: not from absence, but from sovereignty.

That the page is a courtesy: His, answering mine — a not-signing that nevertheless accepted a place in my book, the way a king accepts a chair in someone else's house even if he does not sit.

Or that the page is a mirror, and its blankness says nothing about Him and everything about us, the ones who need things to be on record.

I do not choose. In this book one cannot lie, and choosing without knowing is the oldest lie of the guild.

What I do choose to register is the fact that matters to this chronicle, the fact for which this chapter exists and for which I still exist: in a book where everything can be closed, where the pages go out in the order they were sewn, there is one — the fourth of my living pages — that no hand (and when I say no hand, the reader of this story will very soon know with what exactness I say it) can touch.

The bishops finished their creed and went off to fight over it for the next one thousand seven hundred years. The hall was dismantled in a week. And an old copyist left Nicaea with her donkey and her book, which weighed the same as on the way in plus the exact weight of one blank page, which is not zero.

It is not zero. That is all I know of Him, after twenty-five centuries: that His silence has weight, and that the weight is kind.

On that I have sustained myself. Whether it is enough remains to be seen.
