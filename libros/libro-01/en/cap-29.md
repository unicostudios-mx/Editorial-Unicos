# 29. The Rounds of the Niña Blanca

Santa Muerte came back to the workshop a week after the night of the three-minute rain, without an appointment, at eleven, in her denim jacket, carrying two machine coffees in a cardboard tray.

"I've come to invite you," she said. "On my rounds. You're writing about how we work, the ones from above, no? Then come see how I work. Fair warning: I work nights and on foot. And you don't bring the book open, scribe. Where I work, no one takes notes in front of people."

I went. This chapter is the record of that night, taken from memory — which I have absolute, for better and worse — and written out with her permission, which she gave the way she gives everything: "Write what you saw. Just don't put ornaments on me. My ornaments my muchachos put on me, and theirs come out better."

—

First stop: a public hospital, the emergency corridor, two in the morning. We did not enter through any door; with her, you arrive at places the way one remembers them. In the plastic chairs a family sat waiting for news of a grandfather, and at the edge, alone, a boy in a hoodie with the gaze of those who have already received theirs.

I watched her work. She did nothing. I want it understood with archive precision, what I am saying: *she did nothing*, and she did it for forty minutes. She sat down next to the boy in the hoodie, in a plastic chair, and stayed there, pure presence, while he stared at the floor. The boy did not see her. At some point he breathed deeper, wiped his face with his sleeve, took out his phone and called someone — "amá, it's me, yes, I'm still here" — and then she got up, gave me a sign, and we moved on.

"That's all?" I asked outside.

"That's all." She adjusted her jacket. "Does it seem little to you? You tell me — you buried half your family this month: when you sat six days beside the gardener, what did you do?"

I did not answer. She did not need me to. The young gods ask like the old ones: so that you will hear yourself.

—

We walked her city, which is the same as mine with one more layer on it. An altar on a corner of La Merced, with its glass case and its cheap flowers, where a señora who sells through the night changed the water at three in the morning and told her, in a small voice, about the daughter who doesn't call. A trucker at a stop on the Puebla road, who kept a little figure of her on the dashboard between the Virgin and a photo of his girls, and who before climbing in touched all three with two fingers, evenly, without hierarchies, with that democracy of men who stake their skin by the kilometer. A rooftop room where an older woman kept vigil over her sick son, and prayed to her — to my companion in the denim jacket — not asking for a cure, I listened carefully, with the ear doña Socorro trained: asking *"that when it comes, let it come without pain, Niña Blanca, and let me hold up."*

The second prayer. The one that is granted. All night, at every stop, I did not hear one of the first kind.

With the small hours nearly spent we passed the sidewalk of a reclusorio, where the visitors' line begins forming before dawn: women, nearly all of them, with market bags full of inspectable food and that weatherproof patience which in this city is a construction material. She walked the whole line, slowly, unseen by anyone and with everyone settling slightly as she passed, the way sleeping people settle when someone trusted crosses the room. Before a señora in a brown sweater, who had been coming for years — that shows in the feet, she taught me — she stopped longer.

"That one no longer comes for her son," she told me, low, though there was no need. "Her son got out last year and went back to the same thing. She comes for her son's old cellmate, who has no one. She brings him guisado on Thursdays and calls him sobrino." She adjusted her jacket, with something that in any other muchacha would have been family pride. "Of these I have more than of all the rest put together. Your theologians go looking for my doctrine in my altars. My doctrine is this señora. I am just the one who keeps her company in line."

"Have you noticed?" she said to me on the rooftop, looking out over the city. "Mine don't ask me to keep them from dying. That cannot be asked of me; it would be like asking water not to wet, and my muchachos know it without anyone teaching them. They ask me for the other things: that it not hurt too much, that it not be alone, that there be time enough to say goodbye, that the absence not kill them too." She shrugged. "I don't grant, scribe. I *keep company*. Turns out it was a vacant post. It had been vacant a long time."

And there it stands, for my archive and for the theologians who will someday study the youngest one: the vacancy. The great ones of my book promised harvests, victories, children, heavens. Not one, in seven thousand pages, held as their sole office the final stretch and the ones who walk it. That stretch was left without a window when my inscribed went out, and the people of this land — who lay flowers for death and bake it bread; the deva knew how to read a plaza — manufactured, out of the pure force of their need, the official of the final stretch. It is not mine to judge the cult, and this notebook does not judge: it registers. I register that in the whole night I saw nothing promised, nothing charged, no one threatened. I saw plastic chairs occupied. In my guild, after this spring, that is the highest unit of measure I have.

—

At the last stop — her big altar, the famous one, the sky already going gray — I ventured the question I had been carrying since the workshop:

"The polite señor. The one of the wakes." No name was needed. "Do you know him?"

"He came to see me when I was being born." She said it calmly, arranging some flowers an early devotee had just left. "He introduced himself very properly, as he does. He told me: 'You and I are almost colleagues, muchacha. We both work the end.'" She straightened a veladora. "And I told him no. With respect, because respect I have for him, and you should too: he is serious, and the serious ones are scarce. But I told him no. He is the end. I am the *company* in the end. It is not the same post, scribe. It's not even in the same building."

"And what did he say?"

"He looked at me a long while, like he was weighing me. And once he'd weighed me he said: 'You will last, muchacha. The ones who keep company last longer than the ones who promise.' And he left." She glanced at me sideways, with a young shine. "Is that true, what he said?"

I looked at her altar, the fresh flowers at six in the morning, the señora of La Merced, the trucker, the mother on the rooftop, the boy in the hoodie dialing "amá, I'm still here." I looked at seven thousand pages of closed promises in my bag, and at the only one from above I know whose entire office fits in an occupied plastic chair.

"In my archive," I told her, "it is the truest thing there is."

She walked me out to find a taxi, because she is of the kind who keep you company to the curb. The driver who stopped had her small image hanging from the mirror, next to a rosary and a pine air freshener. He looked at her — a muchacha in a denim jacket at six in the morning — without recognizing her, and said: "Need a ride, jefa?" And she laughed, and told him no, she'd walk, and to drive the señora carefully.

"Always carefully, jefe," she said, closing my door. "You're carrying one who writes. Those are the ones who make us last."
